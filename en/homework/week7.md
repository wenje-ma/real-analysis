> **Basic Knowledge: Intersection and Complement Properties of Rectangle Algebras**<br>For measurable rectangles, $\left(A\times B\right)\cap\left(C\times D\right)=\left(A\cap C\right)\times\left(B\cap D\right)$ and $\left(A\times B\right)^{c}=\left(A^{c}\times B\right)\cup\left(A\times B^{c}\right)$.

> **Basic Knowledge: The Restriction of a Measure to an Algebra is a Premeasure**<br>If $\lambda$ is a measure on a $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal M$ and $\mathcal E\subset\mathcal M$ is an algebra, then $\lambda|_{\mathcal E}$ satisfies the premeasure conditions (empty-set property and $\sigma$-additivity hold within $\mathcal E$).

> **Definition 5.1.2 (Measure)**<br>Let $\left(X,\mathcal F\right)$ be a measurable space. A function $\mu:\mathcal F\to\left[0,+\infty\right]$ is called a measure on $\left(X,\mathcal F\right)$ if: (1) nonnegativity: $\mu\left(E\right)\ge0$ for all $E\in\mathcal F$; (2) empty set: $\mu\left(\emptyset\right)=0$; (3) countable additivity ($\sigma$-additivity): for any countable pairwise-disjoint $\left\{E_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal F$, $\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu\left(E_{n}\right)$.

> **Definition 6.2.1 (Premeasure)**<br>Let $X$ be a nonempty set and $\mathcal E\subset\mathcal P\left(X\right)$ an algebra. A function $\mu_{0}:\mathcal E\to\left[0,+\infty\right]$ is called a premeasure if $\mu_{0}\left(\emptyset\right)=0$, and if $\left\{E_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal E$ are pairwise disjoint with $\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\in\mathcal E$, then $\mu_{0}\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{0}\left(E_{n}\right)$.

> **Theorem 6.3.3 (Carathéodory's Theorem)**<br>Let $\mu^{*}$ be an outer measure on $X$. Then $\mathcal M$ (all $\mu^{*}$-measurable sets) is a $\sigma$-algebra, and $\mu^{*}|_{\mathcal M}$ is a complete measure. If $\mu^{*}$ is generated by a premeasure $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{0}$ is $\sigma$-finite, then $\mu^{*}|_{\mathcal M}$ is the unique extension of $\mu_{0}$.

> **Proposition 7.1.5 (Composition Preserves Measurability)**<br>If $f:X\to Y$ is $\left(\mathcal F,\mathcal G\right)$-measurable and $g:Y\to Z$ is $\left(\mathcal G,\mathcal H\right)$-measurable, then $g\circ f:X\to Z$ is $\left(\mathcal F,\mathcal H\right)$-measurable.

> **Proposition 7.1.7 (Measurability on Product Spaces)**<br>Let $f=\left(f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\right):X\to Y_{1}\times\cdots\times Y_{n}$. With the product $\sigma$-algebra on the product space, $f$ is measurable iff each coordinate map $f_{i}:X\to Y_{i}$ is measurable.

> **Definition 10.1.1 (Product $\sigma$-Algebra)**<br>Let $\left(X,\mathcal A,\mu\right)$ and $\left(Y,\mathcal B,\nu\right)$ be two measure spaces. The product $\sigma$-algebra on $X\times Y$ is $\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B=\sigma\left(\left\{A\times B:A\in\mathcal A,B\in\mathcal B\right\}\right)$, i.e. the $\sigma$-algebra generated by all measurable rectangles.

> **Definition 10.1.2 (Sections)**<br>For $E\subseteq X\times Y$, define its x-section $E_{x}=\left\{y\in Y:\left(x,y\right)\in E\right\}$ and y-section $E^{y}=\left\{x\in X:\left(x,y\right)\in E\right\}$.

> **Theorem 10.1.4 (Existence and Uniqueness of the Product Measure)**<br>Let $\left(X,\mathcal A,\mu\right)$ and $\left(Y,\mathcal B,\nu\right)$ be $\sigma$-finite measure spaces. There is a unique measure $\mu\times\nu$ on $\left(X\times Y,\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B\right)$ such that $\left(\mu\times\nu\right)\left(A\times B\right)=\mu\left(A\right)\nu\left(B\right)$ for all $A\in\mathcal A$, $B\in\mathcal B$. It is constructed as $\phi\left(E\right)=\int_{X}\nu\left(E_{x}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)$ for $E\in\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B$.

> **Theorem 10.2.1 (Fubini's Theorem)**<br>Let $\left(X,\mathcal A,\mu\right)$ and $\left(Y,\mathcal B,\nu\right)$ be $\sigma$-finite measure spaces and $f:X\times Y\to\mathbb R$ be $\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B$-measurable. If $f$ is integrable on $X\times Y$, i.e. $\int_{X\times Y}\left|f\right|\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)< \infty$, then for almost every $x$, $y\mapsto f\left(x,y\right)$ is $\nu$-integrable; for almost every $y$, $x\mapsto f\left(x,y\right)$ is $\mu$-integrable; and the iterated integrals agree:

$$
\int_{X\times Y}f\,\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)=\int_{X}\left(\int_{Y}f\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(y\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)=\int_{Y}\left(\int_{X}f\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(y\right).
$$

> **Theorem 10.2.2 (Tonelli's Theorem)**<br>If $f:X\times Y\to\left[0,\infty\right]$ is $\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B$-measurable, then

$$
\int_{X\times Y}f\,\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)=\int_{X}\left(\int_{Y}f\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(y\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)=\int_{Y}\left(\int_{X}f\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(y\right),
$$

with all integral values in $\left[0,\infty\right]$.

## 1

Let $\mathcal E$ be the collection of all finite disjoint unions of rectangles. Prove that $\mathcal E$ is an algebra. For $E=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\times B_{i}$ (pairwise disjoint), define $\pi\left(E\right):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu\left(A_{i}\right)\nu\left(B_{i}\right)$. Prove that $\pi$ is a premeasure on $\mathcal E$, and that the measure on $\sigma\left(\mathcal E\right)$ induced by this premeasure agrees with the product measure defined in **Theorem 10.1.4 (Existence and Uniqueness of the Product Measure)**.

### Solution 1

**$\mathcal E$ is an algebra.** By **Basic Knowledge: Intersection and Complement Properties of Rectangle Algebras**, the intersection of measurable rectangles is a measurable rectangle, and the complement of a measurable rectangle is a union of two measurable rectangles. The empty set $\emptyset$ and the whole space $X\times Y$ lie in $\mathcal E$. Taking complements, intersections, and unions of elements of $\mathcal E$, first write each element as a finite disjoint union of rectangles; then, since intersections of rectangles are rectangles and after common refinement they are pairwise disjoint, the result is again a finite disjoint union of rectangles, hence in $\mathcal E$. Thus $\mathcal E$ is an algebra.

**$\pi$ is well-defined.** Let $E=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}\times B_{i}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}C_{j}\times D_{j}$ be two pairwise-disjoint rectangle decompositions of $E$. For the common refinement $\left\{\left(A_{i}\cap C_{j}\right)\times\left(B_{i}\cap D_{j}\right)\right\}_{i,j}$, by finite additivity of $\mu,\nu$,

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu\left(A_{i}\right)\nu\left(B_{i}\right)=\sum_{i,j}\mu\left(A_{i}\cap C_{j}\right)\nu\left(B_{i}\cap D_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu\left(C_{j}\right)\nu\left(D_{j}\right),
$$

so $\pi\left(E\right)$ is independent of the decomposition.

**$\pi$ is a premeasure on $\mathcal E$.** By **Theorem 10.1.4 (Existence and Uniqueness of the Product Measure)**, $\mu\times\nu$ is a measure on $\left(X\times Y,\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B\right)$ with $\left(\mu\times\nu\right)\left(A\times B\right)=\mu\left(A\right)\nu\left(B\right)$ on rectangles; for a finite disjoint union $\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\times B_{i}$, by finite additivity of the measure,

$$
\left(\mu\times\nu\right)\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\times B_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu\left(A_{i}\right)\nu\left(B_{i}\right)=\pi\left(E\right),
$$

so $\pi=\left(\mu\times\nu\right)|_{\mathcal E}$. By **Basic Knowledge: The Restriction of a Measure to an Algebra is a Premeasure**, $\pi$ satisfies the premeasure conditions: $\pi\left(\emptyset\right)=0$, and for pairwise disjoint $\left\{E_{n}\right\}\subset\mathcal E$ with $\bigcup_{n}E_{n}\in\mathcal E$, by $\sigma$-additivity of $\mu\times\nu$ (**Definition 5.1.2 (Measure)**),

$$
\pi\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\right)=\left(\mu\times\nu\right)\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)\left(E_{n}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\pi\left(E_{n}\right).
$$

Thus $\pi$ is a premeasure on $\mathcal E$ (**Definition 6.2.1 (Premeasure)**).

**Unique extension agrees with the product measure.** By **Definition 10.1.1 (Product $\sigma$-Algebra)**, $\mathcal E$ is generated by all measurable rectangles, so $\sigma\left(\mathcal E\right)=\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B$. Since $\mu,\nu$ are $\sigma$-finite, take $X=\bigcup_{p}X_{p}$, $Y=\bigcup_{q}Y_{q}$ with $\mu\left(X_{p}\right),\nu\left(Y_{q}\right)< \infty$; then the rectangles $X_{p}\times Y_{q}$ cover $X\times Y$ and $\pi\left(X_{p}\times Y_{q}\right)< \infty$, so $\pi$ is $\sigma$-finite. By **Theorem 6.3.3 (Carathéodory's Theorem)** (unique extension of a premeasure), $\pi$ has a unique extension to $\sigma\left(\mathcal E\right)$. This extension agrees with $\pi=\left(\mu\times\nu\right)|_{\mathcal E}$ on $\mathcal E$, hence equals $\mu\times\nu$ on all of $\sigma\left(\mathcal E\right)=\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B$, i.e. it agrees with the product measure in **Theorem 10.1.4 (Existence and Uniqueness of the Product Measure)**. $\blacksquare$

## 2

Let $X=Y=\left[0,1\right]$, $\mathcal M=\mathcal N=\mathcal B_{\left[0,1\right]}$, $\mu$ be Lebesgue measure and $\nu$ be counting measure. If $D=\left\{\left(x,x\right):x\in\left[0,1\right]\right\}$ is the diagonal in $X\times Y$, then $\iint\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\nu$, $\iint\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,\mathrm{d}\mu$, and $\int\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)$ are not all equal.

### Solution 2

By **Definition 10.1.2 (Sections)**, the x-section of the diagonal $D$ is $D_{x}=\left\{y:\left(x,y\right)\in D\right\}=\left\{x\right\}$, a singleton.

First compute $\iint\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\nu$ (inner integral w.r.t. $\mu$, outer w.r.t. $\nu$). For fixed $y$,

$$
\int_{X}\chi_{D}\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)=\mu\left(\left\{x:\left(x,y\right)\in D\right\}\right)=\mu\left(\left\{y\right\}\right)=0,
$$

since a singleton has Lebesgue measure zero. Hence $\iint\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\nu=\int_{Y}0\,\mathrm{d}\nu=0$.

Next compute $\iint\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,\mathrm{d}\mu$ (inner w.r.t. $\nu$). For fixed $x$,

$$
\int_{Y}\chi_{D}\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(y\right)=\nu\left(D_{x}\right)=\nu\left(\left\{x\right\}\right)=1,
$$

since a singleton has counting measure $1$. Hence $\iint\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{X}1\,\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)=\mu\left(\left[0,1\right]\right)=1$.

Finally, by **Theorem 10.1.4 (Existence and Uniqueness of the Product Measure)** (construction $\phi\left(E\right)=\int\nu\left(E_{x}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)$),

$$
\int\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)=\left(\mu\times\nu\right)\left(D\right)=\int_{X}\nu\left(D_{x}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)=\int_{X}\nu\left(\left\{x\right\}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)=\int_{X}1\,\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)=1.
$$

Thus the three integrals are $0$, $1$, $1$, not all equal; in particular $\iint\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\nu=0\ne1=\iint\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,\mathrm{d}\mu$. The reason is that $\nu$ is not $\sigma$-finite, so the $\sigma$-finiteness hypothesis of **Theorem 10.2.1 (Fubini's Theorem)** fails, and the product integral disagrees with the iterated integral first integrating over $\mu$. $\blacksquare$

## 3

Let $X=Y=\mathbb N$, $\mathcal M=\mathcal N=\mathcal P\left(\mathbb N\right)$, and $\mu=\nu$ be counting measure. Define $f\left(m,n\right)=1$ if $m=n$, $f\left(m,n\right)=-1$ if $m=n+1$, and $f\left(m,n\right)=0$ otherwise. Then $\int\left|f\right|\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)=\infty$, and $\iint f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\nu$ and $\iint f\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,\mathrm{d}\mu$ exist but are not equal.

### Solution 3

Compute $\int\left|f\right|\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)=\sum_{m,n}\left|f\left(m,n\right)\right|$. $f$ takes value $1$ on the diagonal $\left\{\left(m,m\right)\right\}$ and $-1$ on the subdiagonal $\left\{\left(m+1,m\right)\right\}$, so $\left|f\right|$ takes value $1$ at infinitely many points in these two families; hence $\int\left|f\right|\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)=\sum_{m,n}\left|f\left(m,n\right)\right|=\infty$.

Compute $\iint f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\nu$ (inner summation over $\mu$, outer over $\nu$). For fixed $n$, $\sum_{m}f\left(m,n\right)=f\left(n,n\right)+f\left(n+1,n\right)=1+\left(-1\right)=0$, so $\iint f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\nu=\sum_{n}0=0$.

Compute $\iint f\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,\mathrm{d}\mu$ (inner over $\nu$). For fixed $m$, when $m\ge1$, $\sum_{n}f\left(m,n\right)=f\left(m,m\right)+f\left(m,m-1\right)=1+\left(-1\right)=0$; while for $m=0$, $f\left(0,n\right)$ is $1$ only at $n=0$, so the sum is $1$. Hence $\iint f\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\sum_{m}\left(\sum_{n}f\left(m,n\right)\right)=1$.

Thus $\iint f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\nu=0\ne1=\iint f\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,\mathrm{d}\mu$. Since $\int\left|f\right|\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)=\infty$, $f$ is not absolutely integrable, the hypothesis $\int\left|f\right|\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)< \infty$ of **Theorem 10.2.1 (Fubini's Theorem)** fails, and the order of integration cannot be exchanged. $\blacksquare$

## 4

Let $\left(X,\mathcal M,\mu\right)$ be a $\sigma$-finite measure space and $f\in L^{+}\left(X\right)$. Set $G_{f}=\left\{\left(x,y\right)\in X\times\left[0,\infty\right]:y\le f\left(x\right)\right\}$. Then $G_{f}$ is $\mathcal M\otimes\mathcal B_{\mathbb R}$-measurable and $\mu\times m\left(G_{f}\right)=\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu$; if the inequality $y\le f\left(x\right)$ is replaced by $y< f\left(x\right)$, the conclusion still holds.

### Solution 4

**Measurability.** Consider the map $T:X\times\left[0,\infty\right]\to\left[0,\infty\right]\times\left[0,\infty\right]$, $T\left(x,y\right)=\left(f\left(x\right),y\right)$. By **Proposition 7.1.7 (Measurability on Product Spaces)**, $T$ is measurable iff its coordinate functions are measurable; the coordinate $\left(x,y\right)\mapsto f\left(x\right)=f\circ p_{1}$ is measurable (since $f$ is measurable), and $\left(x,y\right)\mapsto y=p_{2}$ is continuous hence measurable. Thus $T$ is measurable. Also $\Phi\left(z,y\right)=z-y$ is continuous, hence Borel measurable. By **Proposition 7.1.5 (Composition Preserves Measurability)**, $\left(x,y\right)\mapsto f\left(x\right)-y=\Phi\circ T$ is measurable. Since

$$
G_{f}=\left\{\left(x,y\right):f\left(x\right)-y\ge0\right\}=\left(f-y\right)^{-1}\left(\left[0,\infty\right]\right),
$$

a preimage of a measurable function, by **Definition 10.1.1 (Product $\sigma$-Algebra)**, $G_{f}\in\mathcal M\otimes\mathcal B_{\mathbb R}$.

**Measure value.** By **Theorem 10.2.2 (Tonelli's Theorem)** (nonnegative characteristic function, $\mu\times m$ is $\sigma$-finite),

$$
\left(\mu\times m\right)\left(G_{f}\right)=\int_{X}m\left(\left(G_{f}\right)_{x}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right),
$$

where $\left(G_{f}\right)_{x}=\left\{y\in\left[0,\infty\right]:y\le f\left(x\right)\right\}=\left[0,f\left(x\right)\right]$, and Lebesgue measure $m\left(\left[0,f\left(x\right)\right]\right)=f\left(x\right)$. Hence $\left(\mu\times m\right)\left(G_{f}\right)=\int_{X}f\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)$.

If $y\le f\left(x\right)$ is replaced by $y< f\left(x\right)$, then $\left(G_{f}\right)_{x}=\left[0,f\left(x\right)\right)$, still with $m=f\left(x\right)$, and the conclusion is unchanged. $\blacksquare$

> **Definition 4.1.1** (Algebra of Sets)<br>Let $X$ be a nonempty set and $\mathcal A\subset\mathcal P\left(X\right)$. $\mathcal A$ is called an **algebra of sets** on $X$ if it satisfies:
> (1) $X\in\mathcal A$;
> 
> (2) closed under set difference: $A,B\in\mathcal A\Rightarrow A\setminus B\in\mathcal A$;
> 
> (3) closed under finite unions: $A,B\in\mathcal A\Rightarrow A\cup B\in\mathcal A$.

> **Definition 4.1.3** ($\sigma$-Algebra)<br>Let $X$ be nonempty and $\mathcal F\subset\mathcal P\left(X\right)$. $\mathcal F$ is called a **$\sigma$-algebra** on $X$ if it satisfies:
> (1) $X\in\mathcal F$;
> 
> (2) closed under complementation: $A\in\mathcal F\Rightarrow A^{c}\in\mathcal F$;
> 
> (3) closed under countable unions: $\left\{A_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal F\Rightarrow\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\in\mathcal F$.

> **Definition 4.4.1** (Monotone Class)<br>Let $\mathcal M\subset\mathcal P\left(X\right)$. $\mathcal M$ is called a **monotone class** if it satisfies:
> (1) closed under monotonically increasing sequences: $A_{1}\subset A_{2}\subset\cdots$ and $A_{n}\in\mathcal M\Rightarrow\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\in\mathcal M$;
> 
> (2) closed under monotonically decreasing sequences: $A_{1}\supset A_{2}\supset\cdots$ and $A_{n}\in\mathcal M\Rightarrow\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\in\mathcal M$.

> **Theorem 4.4.2** (Monotone Class Theorem)<br>Let $\mathcal A$ be an algebra and $\mathcal M$ a monotone class containing $\mathcal A$. Then $\sigma\left(\mathcal A\right)\subset\mathcal M$.

> **Definition 5.1.2** (Measure)<br>Let $\left(X,\mathcal F\right)$ be a measurable space. A function $\mu:\mathcal F\to\left[0,+\infty\right]$ is called a **measure** on $\left(X,\mathcal F\right)$ if it satisfies:
> (1) nonnegativity: $\mu\left(E\right)\ge0$ for all $E\in\mathcal F$;
> 
> (2) the empty set has measure zero: $\mu\left(\varnothing\right)=0$;
> 
> (3) countable additivity: for any countably many pairwise disjoint sets $\left\{E_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal F$, we have $\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu\left(E_{n}\right)$.

> **Proposition 5.2.1** (Finite Additivity)<br>Let $\mu$ be a measure and $E_{1},\dots,E_{n}\in\mathcal F$ be pairwise disjoint. Then $\mu\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}E_{k}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu\left(E_{k}\right)$.

> **Proposition 5.2.3** (Countable Subadditivity)<br>Let $\mu$ be a measure and $\left\{E_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ arbitrary. Then $\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\right)\le\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu\left(E_{n}\right)$.

> **Proposition 5.2.4** (Continuity from Below)<br>Let $\mu$ be a measure and $\left\{E_{n}\right\}$ monotonically increasing. Then $\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu\left(E_{n}\right)$.

> **Definition 6.1.1** (Outer Measure)<br>Let $X$ be nonempty. A function $\mu^{*}:\mathcal P\left(X\right)\to\left[0,+\infty\right]$ is called an **outer measure** on $X$ if it satisfies:
> (1) nonnegativity: $\mu^{*}\left(\varnothing\right)=0$, and $\mu^{*}\left(E\right)\ge0$ for all $E\subset X$;
> 
> (2) monotonicity: $E\subset F\Rightarrow\mu^{*}\left(E\right)\le\mu^{*}\left(F\right)$;
> 
> (3) countable subadditivity: $\mu^{*}\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\right)\le\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu^{*}\left(E_{n}\right)$.

> **Definition 6.2.1** (Premeasure)<br>Let $X$ be nonempty and $\mathcal E\subset\mathcal P\left(X\right)$ be an algebra. A function $\mu_{0}:\mathcal E\to\left[0,+\infty\right]$ is called a **premeasure** if:
> (1) $\mu_{0}\left(\varnothing\right)=0$;
> 
> (2) if $\left\{E_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal E$ are pairwise disjoint and $\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\in\mathcal E$, then $\mu_{0}\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{0}\left(E_{n}\right)$.

> **Theorem 6.2.2** (Outer Measure Generated by a Premeasure)<br>Let $\mathcal E\subset\mathcal P\left(X\right)$ be a covering class and $\mu_{0}:\mathcal E\to\left[0,+\infty\right]$ a premeasure. For any $A\subset X$, define $$\mu^{*}\left(A\right)=\inf\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{0}\left(E_{n}\right):E_{n}\in\mathcal E,A\subset\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\right\}.$$ Then $\mu^{*}$ is an outer measure on $X$, and $\mu^{*}|_{\mathcal E}=\mu_{0}$.

> **Definition 6.3.1** (Carathéodory Condition)<br>Let $\mu^{*}$ be an outer measure on $X$. A subset $A\subset X$ is called $\mu^{*}$-**measurable** if, for every $T\subset X$, $$\mu^{*}\left(T\right)=\mu^{*}\left(T\cap A\right)+\mu^{*}\left(T\cap A^{c}\right).$$ The collection of all $\mu^{*}$-measurable sets is denoted $\mathcal M_{\mu^{*}}$.

> **Lemma 6.3.2**<br>If $\mathcal E$ is an algebra and $\mu_{0}$ is a premeasure, then $\mathcal E\subset\mathcal M_{\mu^{*}}$.

> **Theorem 6.3.3** (Carathéodory Theorem)<br>Let $\mu^{*}$ be an outer measure on $X$. Then:
> (1) $\mathcal M$ is a $\sigma$-algebra;
> 
> (2) $\mu^{*}|_{\mathcal M}$ is a complete measure;
> 
> (3) if $\mu^{*}$ is generated by a premeasure $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{0}$ is $\sigma$-finite, then $\mu^{*}|_{\mathcal M}$ is the unique extension of $\mu_{0}$.

> **Definition 6.4.1** (h-Interval)<br>Let $-\infty\le a< b< \infty$. All left-open right-closed intervals $\left(a,b\right]$, as well as $\left(a,\infty\right)$ and $\varnothing$, are called **h-intervals**. The family $\mathcal E$ formed by finite disjoint unions of h-intervals is an algebra.

> **Proposition 6.4.2** (A Premeasure on $\mathcal E$)<br>Let $F:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ be an increasing right-continuous function. For disjoint intervals $\left(a_{i},b_{i}\right]$, $i=1,\dots,n$, define $\mu_{0}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\left(a_{i},b_{i}\right]\right):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[F\left(b_{i}\right)-F\left(a_{i}\right)\right]$, and set $\mu_{0}\left(\varnothing\right)=0$. Then $\mu_{0}$ is a premeasure on $\mathcal E$.

> **Theorem 6.4.5** (Regularity of the Lebesgue–Stieltjes Measure)<br>Let $F:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ be a right-continuous monotonically increasing function, let $\mu_{F}$ be the Lebesgue–Stieltjes measure generated by $F$, and let $\mathcal M^{*}$ be the $\sigma$-algebra of $\mu_{F}$-measurable sets. If $E\in\mathcal M^{*}$, then $$\mu_{F}\left(E\right)=\inf\left\{\mu_{F}\left(U\right):E\subset U,U\text{开}\right\}=\sup\left\{\mu_{F}\left(K\right):K\subset E,K\text{紧}\right\}.$$

> **Definition 6.4.6** (Lebesgue Measure)<br>The measure generated by $F\left(x\right)=x$ is called the **Lebesgue measure**, denoted $m$; the measurable sets defined by the corresponding outer measure are called **Lebesgue measurable sets**, and their collection is denoted $\mathcal L$.

> **Definition 6.5.1** (Recursive Construction of the Cantor Set)<br>Let $C_{0}=\left[0,1\right]$. For each $n\ge0$, define $C_{n+1}=\frac{1}{3}C_{n}\cup\left(\frac{2}{3}+\frac{1}{3}C_{n}\right)$. Then the **Cantor set** is defined as $C=\bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}$.

> **Theorem 6.5.2** (Ternary Characterization of the Cantor Set)<br>A point $x\in\left[0,1\right]$ belongs to the Cantor set $C$ if and only if its ternary expansion $x=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{3^{k}}$ contains no digit $1$ (i.e. $a_{k}\in\left\{0,2\right\}$).

> **Basic Knowledge**<br>**Generalized Cantor set**: let $\left\{\alpha_{j}\right\}\subset\left(0,1\right)$. Starting from $\left[0,1\right]$, at the $j$-th step remove the middle $\alpha_{j}$ portion of each remaining closed interval; the resulting intersection $K=\bigcap_{j}K_{j}$ is called a generalized Cantor set. It satisfies $m\left(K\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\alpha_{j}\right)$, and for any $\beta\in\left(0,1\right)$ one may choose $\left\{\alpha_{j}\right\}$ so that $m\left(K\right)=\beta$; like the ordinary Cantor set, a generalized Cantor set is a closed set that is compact, nowhere dense, and has no isolated points.

### Solution to Problem 1

**(1) Uniqueness.** By **Definition 6.2.1**, $\mu_{0}$ is a premeasure on the algebra $\mathcal E$. We first show that $\mu^{*}|_{\mathcal M}$ is indeed an extension of $\mu_{0}$: by **Theorem 6.2.2**, $\mu^{*}|_{\mathcal E}=\mu_{0}$; by **Lemma 6.3.2**, $\mathcal E\subset\mathcal M$; by **Theorem 6.3.3**(1)(2), $\mathcal M$ is a $\sigma$-algebra and $\mu^{*}|_{\mathcal M}$ is a complete measure. Hence $\mu^{*}|_{\mathcal M}$ is a measure extension of $\mu_{0}$ to $\mathcal M$.

We now prove uniqueness. Let $\nu$ be any measure on $\mathcal M$ satisfying $\nu|_{\mathcal E}=\mu_{0}$; we prove that $\nu=\mu^{*}|_{\mathcal M}$. First assume $\mu_{0}\left(X\right)< \infty$.

**Step 1: $\nu\le\mu^{*}$ on $\mathcal M$.** For any $A\in\mathcal M$ and $\varepsilon> 0$, by the definition of $\mu^{*}$ in **Theorem 6.2.2**, there exists $\left\{E_{n}\right\}\subset\mathcal E$ covering $A$ such that $\sum_{n}\mu_{0}\left(E_{n}\right)\le\mu^{*}\left(A\right)+\varepsilon$. By the countable subadditivity of $\nu$ (**Proposition 5.2.3**) and $\nu|_{\mathcal E}=\mu_{0}$,

$$
\nu\left(A\right)\le\sum_{n}\nu\left(E_{n}\right)=\sum_{n}\mu_{0}\left(E_{n}\right)\le\mu^{*}\left(A\right)+\varepsilon.
$$

Since $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary, $\nu\left(A\right)\le\mu^{*}\left(A\right)$.

**Step 2: $\nu=\mu^{*}$ on $\sigma\left(\mathcal E\right)$.** Set $\mathcal H=\left\{A\in\sigma\left(\mathcal E\right):\nu\left(A\right)=\mu^{*}\left(A\right)\right\}$. For $E\in\mathcal E$, by **Theorem 6.2.2** and $\nu|_{\mathcal E}=\mu_{0}$ we have $\mu^{*}\left(E\right)=\mu_{0}\left(E\right)=\nu\left(E\right)$, so $\mathcal E\subset\mathcal H$. We verify that $\mathcal H$ is a monotone class (**Definition 4.4.1**): if $\left\{A_{n}\right\}\subset\mathcal H$ and $A_{n}\uparrow A$, then by the continuity from below of $\nu,\mu^{*}|_{\mathcal M}$ (**Proposition 5.2.4**; $\sigma\left(\mathcal E\right)\subset\mathcal M$) we get $\nu\left(A\right)=\lim_{n}\nu\left(A_{n}\right)=\lim_{n}\mu^{*}\left(A_{n}\right)=\mu^{*}\left(A\right)$, so $A\in\mathcal H$; the decreasing case is analogous (here $\mu^{*}\left(A_{1}\right)\le\mu^{*}\left(X\right)=\mu_{0}\left(X\right)< \infty$, so continuity from above applies). Thus $\mathcal H$ is a monotone class containing the algebra $\mathcal E$, and by **Theorem 4.4.2** (Monotone Class Theorem), $\sigma\left(\mathcal E\right)\subset\mathcal H$, i.e. $\nu=\mu^{*}$ on $\sigma\left(\mathcal E\right)$.

**Step 3: $\nu=\mu^{*}$ on $\mathcal M$.** For any $A\in\mathcal M$, Step 1 gives $\nu\left(A\right)\le\mu^{*}\left(A\right)$. Also, since $X\in\mathcal E$ (**Definition 4.1.1**), $\nu\left(X\right)=\mu_{0}\left(X\right)=\mu^{*}\left(X\right)$. Applying Step 1 to $A^{c}\in\mathcal M$ (since $\mathcal M$ is a $\sigma$-algebra, **Theorem 6.3.3**(1)), and using $A\in\mathcal M$ gives $\mu^{*}\left(X\right)=\mu^{*}\left(A\right)+\mu^{*}\left(A^{c}\right)$ (the Carathéodory condition at $T=X$),

$$
\nu\left(A\right)=\nu\left(X\right)-\nu\left(A^{c}\right)=\mu^{*}\left(X\right)-\nu\left(A^{c}\right)\ge\mu^{*}\left(X\right)-\mu^{*}\left(A^{c}\right)=\mu^{*}\left(A\right).
$$

Hence $\nu\left(A\right)=\mu^{*}\left(A\right)$. Thus uniqueness is proved when $\mu_{0}\left(X\right)< \infty$.

**The $\sigma$-finite case.** Suppose $\mu_{0}$ is $\sigma$-finite, i.e. there exist pairwise disjoint $X_{k}\in\mathcal E$ such that $X=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}X_{k}$ and $\mu_{0}\left(X_{k}\right)< \infty$. For any $A\in\mathcal M$, by countable additivity and continuity from below,

$$
\mu^{*}\left(A\right)=\sum_{k}\mu^{*}\left(A\cap X_{k}\right),;\nu\left(A\right)=\sum_{k}\nu\left(A\cap X_{k}\right).
$$

For each $k$, restricting the finite measures $\mu_{0}\left(\cdot\cap X_{k}\right)$ and $\nu\left(\cdot\cap X_{k}\right)$ to $X_{k}$ reduces to the finite case, giving $\nu\left(A\cap X_{k}\right)=\mu^{*}\left(A\cap X_{k}\right)$. Summing gives $\nu\left(A\right)=\mu^{*}\left(A\right)$. Hence $\mu^{*}|_{\mathcal M}$ is the unique extension of $\mu_{0}$ to $\mathcal M$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 2

Let $-\infty\le a< b< \infty$. All left-open right-closed intervals $\left(a,b\right]$, as well as $\left(a,\infty\right)$ and $\varnothing$, are called h-intervals. Prove that the family $\mathcal E$ formed by finite disjoint unions of h-intervals is an algebra (**Definition 6.4.1**).

### Solution to Problem 2

We verify using **Definition 4.1.1** (Algebra of Sets).

We first show that the intersection of two h-intervals is again an h-interval. Indeed, $\left(a,b\right]\cap\left(c,d\right]=\left(\max\left(a,c\right),\min\left(b,d\right)\right]$ (if nonempty), $\left(a,b\right]\cap\left(c,\infty\right)=\left(\max\left(a,c\right),b\right]$, $\left(a,\infty\right)\cap\left(c,\infty\right)=\left(\max\left(a,c\right),\infty\right)$, and the intersection with $\varnothing$ is $\varnothing$. Hence the intersection of h-intervals is an h-interval. It follows that the intersection of any two finite disjoint unions in $\mathcal E$ can still be expanded into a disjoint union of finitely many h-intervals, so $\mathcal E$ is closed under finite intersections.

We next show that $\mathcal E$ is closed under complementation. The complement of a single h-interval:

$$
\varnothing^{c}=\mathbb R=\left(-\infty,\infty\right),;\left(a,\infty\right)^{c}=\left(-\infty,a\right],;\left(a,b\right]^{c}=\left(-\infty,a\right]\cup\left(b,\infty\right),
$$

where $\left(-\infty,a\right]=\left(-\infty,a\right]$ and $\left(b,\infty\right)$ are both h-intervals, and they are pairwise disjoint. Hence the complement of each h-interval still belongs to $\mathcal E$. For an element $A=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}I_{i}$ of $\mathcal E$ ($I_{i}$ being disjoint h-intervals), by De Morgan's laws

$$
A^{c}=\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}I_{i}\right)^{c}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}I_{i}^{c}\in\mathcal E,
$$

since each $I_{i}^{c}\in\mathcal E$ and $\mathcal E$ is closed under finite intersections.

Finally, $X\in\mathcal E$: since $\mathbb R=\left(-\infty,\infty\right)=\left(-\infty,\infty\right)$ is an h-interval of type $\left(a,\infty\right)$ ($a=-\infty$), we have $\mathbb R\in\mathcal E$. Moreover, for $A,B\in\mathcal E$, by De Morgan's laws $A\cup B=\left(A^{c}\cap B^{c}\right)^{c}\in\mathcal E$. Hence $\mathcal E$ is closed under finite unions.

In summary, $\mathcal E$ is an algebra. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 3

The Lebesgue measure $m$ constructed from $F\left(x\right)=x$ (**Definition 6.4.6**) satisfies:

(1) $m$ is translation-invariant: $m\left(E+x\right)=m\left(E\right)$ for all measurable sets $E$ and $x\in\mathbb R$;

(2) $m$ is scale-invariant: $m\left(rE\right)=\left|r\right|m\left(E\right)$ for all measurable sets $E$ and $r\in\mathbb R$.

(i.e. parts (1)(2) of **Theorem 6.4.7**.)

### Solution to Problem 3

Let $\mathcal E$ be the algebra of h-intervals, and let $\mu_{0}$ be the premeasure defined from $F\left(x\right)=x$ via **Proposition 6.4.2**, i.e. for an h-interval $\mu_{0}\left(\left(a,b\right]\right)=b-a$ (the interval length); by **Theorem 6.2.2**, $\mu_{0}$ generates the outer measure

$$
m^{*}\left(A\right)=\inf\left\{\sum_{n}\left(b_{n}-a_{n}\right):A\subset\bigcup_{n}\left(a_{n},b_{n}\right]\right\},
$$

and the Lebesgue measure is $m=m^{*}|_{\mathcal L}$ (**Definition 6.4.6**).

(1) We first prove that the outer measure is translation-invariant. For $A\subset\mathbb R$ and $x\in\mathbb R$, the translation $\tau_{x}:t\mapsto t+x$ maps h-intervals to h-intervals and preserves length: $\mu_{0}\left(I+x\right)=\mu_{0}\left(I\right)$. Hence $\left\{I_{n}\right\}$ covers $A$ if and only if $\left\{I_{n}+x\right\}$ covers $A+x$, and

$$
\sum_{n}\mu_{0}\left(I_{n}+x\right)=\sum_{n}\mu_{0}\left(I_{n}\right).
$$

By the definition of $m^{*}$ (the infimum over covers), we get $m^{*}\left(A+x\right)=m^{*}\left(A\right)$.

We next prove that measurability is preserved under translation. If $E\in\mathcal L$ (i.e. $E$ is $m^{*}$-measurable, **Definition 6.3.1**), then for any $T\subset\mathbb R$,

$$
m^{*}\left(T\right)=m^{*}\left(T-x\right)=m^{*}\left(\left(T-x\right)\cap E\right)+m^{*}\left(\left(T-x\right)\cap E^{c}\right)=m^{*}\left(T\cap\left(E+x\right)\right)+m^{*}\left(T\cap\left(E+x\right)^{c}\right),
$$

where in the second equality we apply the measurability of $E$ at $T-x$. Hence $E+x$ is measurable. Thus for a measurable set $E$,

$$
m\left(E+x\right)=m^{*}\left(E+x\right)=m^{*}\left(E\right)=m\left(E\right).
$$

(1) is proved.

(2) For $r\ne0$, the dilation $\delta_{r}:t\mapsto rt$ maps an h-interval $\left(a,b\right]$ to $\left(ra,rb\right]$ ($r> 0$) or $\left(rb,ra\right]$ ($r< 0$), which is again an h-interval, and its length becomes $\left|r\right|\left(b-a\right)$, i.e. $\mu_{0}\left(rI\right)=\left|r\right|\mu_{0}\left(I\right)$. By the same argument as in (1), $m^{*}\left(rA\right)=\left|r\right|m^{*}\left(A\right)$, and $E$ measurable implies $rE$ measurable (using the Carathéodory condition and the dilation). Hence $m\left(rE\right)=\left|r\right|m\left(E\right)$. When $r=0$, $0\cdot E$ is either empty or $\left\{0\right\}$, and both have measure $0=\left|0\right|m\left(E\right)$. (2) is proved. $\blacksquare$

By **Theorem 6.5.2**, $x\in C$ if and only if the ternary expansion $x=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{3^{k}}$ of $x$ satisfies $a_{k}\in\left\{0,2\right\}$.

**1. Compactness.** By **Definition 6.5.1**, each $C_{n}$ is obtained from $\left[0,1\right]$ by removing finitely many open intervals (the open intervals dug out at the $n$-th step), so $C_{n}$ is a finite union of closed intervals and hence closed. $C=\bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}$ is an intersection of closed sets, so $C$ is closed, and $C\subset\left[0,1\right]$ is bounded. By the Heine–Borel theorem, closed bounded sets in $\mathbb R$ are compact, so $C$ is compact. $\blacksquare$

**2. Completeness.** $\mathbb R$ is a complete metric space, and $C$ is a closed subset of $\mathbb R$. If $\left\{x_{n}\right\}\subset C$ is a Cauchy sequence, then it converges in $\mathbb R$ to some $x$; since $C$ is closed, $x\in C$. Hence every Cauchy sequence in $C$ converges, and $C$ is complete. $\blacksquare$

**3. Total disconnectedness.** Suppose $K\subset C$ is connected and contains two points $x< y$. By **Theorem 6.5.2**, the ternary expansions of $x,y$ contain no digit $1$. Since $x\ne y$, there is a smallest $n$ at which the $n$-th ternary digit of $x,y$ differ; without loss of generality, the $n$-th digit of $x$ is $0$, and the $n$-th digit of $y$ is $2$ (the first $n-1$ digits being the same). Then one of the open intervals dug out at the $n$-th step, say $J=\left(\alpha,\beta\right)$, lies between $x$ and $y$ (i.e. $x< \alpha< \beta< y$), and $J\cap C=\varnothing$. Thus

$$
K=\left(K\cap\left(-\infty,\alpha\right]\right)\cup\left(K\cap\left[\beta,\infty\right)\right),
$$

is a union of two nonempty, disjoint $K$-relative open sets (since $K\subset C$ and $K\cap J=\varnothing$), contradicting the connectedness of $K$. Hence $K$ can only be a singleton. $\blacksquare$

**4. Nowhere dense.** Take any nonempty open interval $\left(\alpha,\beta\right)\subset\mathbb R$. If it intersects $\left[0,1\right]$, choose $n$ such that $3^{-n}< \beta-\alpha$. After the $n$-th step, $C_{n}$ is a union of $2^{n}$ closed intervals of length $3^{-n}$; the $2^{n}-1$ open intervals between these closed intervals (dug out at the $n$-th step) cover the part of $\left[0,1\right]$ except the endpoints, and all the dug-out intervals have length $3^{-n}$. Since $\left(\alpha,\beta\right)$ has length greater than $3^{-n}$, it must contain some open interval dug out at the $n$-th step, so $\left(\alpha,\beta\right)$ is not entirely contained in $C$. Hence $C$ contains no nonempty open interval, $\operatorname{int}\left(C\right)=\varnothing$, i.e. $C$ is nowhere dense. $\blacksquare$

**5. Perfect set.** That it is closed was already proved in (1). We prove that $C$ has no isolated points: let $x\in C$. For any $\varepsilon> 0$, by **Theorem 6.5.2** write $x=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{3^{k}}$ with $a_{k}\in\left\{0,2\right\}$. Choose $n$ such that $\frac{2}{3^{n+1}}< \varepsilon$, and let $x_{n}$ be the point obtained from the ternary expansion of $x$ by replacing the $n+1$-th digit $a_{n+1}$ with $2-a_{n+1}$ and leaving all other digits unchanged. Then $x_{n}\in C$ (its ternary expansion contains no $1$), $x_{n}\ne x$, and

$$
\left|x_{n}-x\right|=\left|\frac{2-a_{n+1}}{3^{n+1}}-\frac{a_{n+1}}{3^{n+1}}\right|=\frac{2}{3^{n+1}}< \varepsilon.
$$

Hence the $\varepsilon$-neighborhood of $x$ contains points of $C$ other than $x$, so $x$ is not an isolated point. Therefore $C$ is a perfect set. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 5

Prove Problems 30 and 33.

**30.** Let $E\in\mathcal L$ and $m\left(E\right)> 0$. Prove that for any $\alpha< 1$, there exists an open interval $I$ such that $m\left(E\cap I\right)> \alpha m\left(I\right)$.

**33.** Prove that there exists a Borel set $A\subset\left[0,1\right]$ such that for every subinterval $I$ of $\left[0,1\right]$, $0< m\left(A\cap I\right)< m\left(I\right)$. (Hint: every subinterval of $\left[0,1\right]$ contains a Cantor-type set of positive measure.)

### Solution to Problem 5

**30.** Proof by contradiction. Suppose there exists $\alpha< 1$ such that for every open interval $I$, $m\left(E\cap I\right)\le\alpha m\left(I\right)$. Then for any open set $U$, write $U$ as a union of countably many pairwise disjoint open intervals $U=\bigcup_{k}I_{k}$; by the countable additivity of $m$,

$$
m\left(E\cap U\right)=\sum_{k}m\left(E\cap I_{k}\right)\le\alpha\sum_{k}m\left(I_{k}\right)=\alpha m\left(U\right).
$$

By the outer regularity of the Lebesgue–Stieltjes measure (**Theorem 6.4.5**), $m\left(E\right)=\inf\left\{m\left(U\right):E\subset U\text{开}\right\}$. Take a sequence of open sets $U_{n}\supset E$ such that $m\left(U_{n}\right)\to m\left(E\right)$. Since $E\subset U_{n}$, $E=E\cap U_{n}$, hence

$$
m\left(E\right)=m\left(E\cap U_{n}\right)\le\alpha m\left(U_{n}\right)\to\alpha m\left(E\right).
$$

Thus $m\left(E\right)\le\alpha m\left(E\right)$. Since $m\left(E\right)> 0$, we get $\alpha\ge1$, contradicting $\alpha< 1$. Hence there exists an open interval $I$ such that $m\left(E\cap I\right)> \alpha m\left(I\right)$. $\blacksquare$

**33.** Use generalized Cantor sets of positive measure (Basic Knowledge). Let $\left\{I_{n}\right\}$ be an enumeration of all open intervals in $\left[0,1\right]$ with rational endpoints. Inductively choose pairwise disjoint closed intervals $J_{n}\subset I_{n}$ (choosing $J_{n}$ to avoid all previous $J_{k}$) so that every subinterval $I$ contains some $J_{n}$ (since $\left\{I_{n}\right\}$ enumerates the rational intervals, every subinterval contains some $I_{n}$, and $J_{n}\subset I_{n}$). Fix $c\in\left(0,1\right)$. Inside each $J_{n}$, take a generalized Cantor set $C_{n}\subset J_{n}$ such that $m\left(C_{n}\right)=c\,m\left(J_{n}\right)$ (a generalized Cantor set can have any measure $\beta\in\left(0,1\right)$). Since the $J_{n}$ are pairwise disjoint, the $C_{n}$ are pairwise disjoint. Set $A=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}C_{n}$; then $A$ is a countable union of closed sets, hence a Borel set, and $A\subset\left[0,1\right]$.

We first prove the lower bound. For any subinterval $I\subset\left[0,1\right]$, there exists $n$ such that $J_{n}\subset I_{n}\subset I$, hence

$$
m\left(A\cap I\right)\ge m\left(A\cap J_{n}\right)=m\left(C_{n}\right)=c\,m\left(J_{n}\right)> 0.
$$

We next prove the upper bound. Since $C_{n}\subset J_{n}$ and the $\left\{J_{n}\right\}$ are pairwise disjoint,

$$
m\left(A\cap I\right)=m\left(\bigcup_{n}C_{n}\cap I\right)=\sum_{n}m\left(C_{n}\cap I\right)=\sum_{J_{n}\subset I}m\left(C_{n}\right)=c\sum_{J_{n}\subset I}m\left(J_{n}\right)=c\,m\left(\bigcup_{J_{n}\subset I}J_{n}\right)\le c\,m\left(I\right)< m\left(I\right),
$$

where $m\left(C_{n}\cap I\right)=m\left(C_{n}\right)$ is nonzero only when $J_{n}\cap I$ has positive measure (i.e. $J_{n}\subset I$, differing at most at the endpoints). Hence for every subinterval $I$ of $\left[0,1\right]$, $0< m\left(A\cap I\right)< m\left(I\right)$. $\blacksquare$

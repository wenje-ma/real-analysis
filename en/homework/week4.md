> **Definition 4.1.3** ($\sigma$-Algebra)<br>Let $X$ be nonempty and $\mathcal F\subset\mathcal P\left(X\right)$. $\mathcal F$ is called a **$\sigma$-algebra** on $X$ if it satisfies:
> (1) $X\in\mathcal F$;
>
> (2) closed under complementation: $A\in\mathcal F\Rightarrow A^{c}\in\mathcal F$;
>
> (3) closed under countable unions: $\left\{A_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal F\Rightarrow\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\in\mathcal F$.

> **Definition 4.3.2** (Borel $\sigma$-Algebra)<br>Let $\left(X,\tau\right)$ be a topological space. The $\sigma$-algebra generated by all open sets is called the **Borel $\sigma$-algebra**, denoted $\mathcal B\left(X\right)$; its elements are called **Borel sets**.

> **Definition 4.5.2** (Product $\sigma$-Algebra)<br>The product $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal F\otimes\mathcal G$ is the smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing all measurable rectangles: $\mathcal F\otimes\mathcal G:=\sigma\left(\left\{A\times B:A\in\mathcal F,B\in\mathcal G\right\}\right)$.

> **Theorem 4.5.4** (Products of Borel Sets)<br>For the Borel $\sigma$-algebras on Euclidean spaces, we have $\mathcal B\left(\mathbb R^{m}\right)\otimes\mathcal B\left(\mathbb R^{n}\right)=\mathcal B\left(\mathbb R^{m+n}\right)$.

> **Definition 7.1.1** (Measurable Mapping)<br>Let $\left(X,\mathcal F\right)$ and $\left(Y,\mathcal G\right)$ be measurable spaces. A map $f:X\to Y$ is called $\left(\mathcal F,\mathcal G\right)$-**measurable** if, for every $B\in\mathcal G$, $f^{-1}\left(B\right)\in\mathcal F$.

> **Proposition 7.1.7** (Measurability on Product Spaces)<br>Let $f=\left(f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\right):X\to Y_{1}\times\cdots\times Y_{n}$. With the product space equipped with the product $\sigma$-algebra, $f$ is measurable if and only if each coordinate map $f_{i}:X\to Y_{i}$ is measurable.

> **Theorem 7.1.8** (Closure Properties of Real-Valued Measurable Functions)<br>Let $f,g:X\to\mathbb R$ be measurable and $c\in\mathbb R$. Then:
> (1) $cf$, $f+g$, $fg$, $\max\left(f,g\right)$, $\min\left(f,g\right)$ are measurable;
>
> (2) if $g\left(x\right)\ne0$ for all $x$, then $f/g$ is measurable;
>
> (3) if $\left\{f_{n}\right\}$ are measurable, then $\sup_{n}f_{n}$, $\inf_{n}f_{n}$, $\limsup_{n\to\infty}f_{n}$, $\liminf_{n\to\infty}f_{n}$ are measurable;
>
> (4) if $\left\{f_{n}\right\}$ converges pointwise, then the limit function $\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}$ is measurable.

> **Theorem 7.1.9** (Composition of a Measurable Function and a Continuous Function)<br>Let $f:X\to\mathbb R$ be measurable and $g:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ continuous. Then $g\circ f:X\to\mathbb R$ is measurable.

> **Example 7.3.5** (Indicator Function)<br>Let $A\subset X$. Then $\chi_{A}:X\to\mathbb R$ is measurable if and only if $A\in\mathcal F$.

> **Basic Knowledge**<br>**Existence of a Lebesgue non-measurable set** (Vitali set): in the Lebesgue measure space $\left(\mathbb R,\mathcal L,m\right)$, there exists $A\subset\mathbb R$ such that $A\notin\mathcal L$. Its construction is based on the axiom of choice: choosing one representative from each equivalence class of $\mathbb R/\mathbb Q$ yields a Lebesgue non-measurable set.

### Problem 1

Let $\left(X,\mathcal M\right)$ be a measurable space and $\left\{f_{n}\right\}$ a sequence of measurable functions. Prove the following propositions (Problems 3, 6, 11).

**3.** If $\left\{f_{n}\right\}$ is a sequence of measurable functions on $X$, then $\left\{x:\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}\left(x\right)\text{存在}\right\}$ is a measurable set.

**6.** Prove that the supremum of uncountably many measurable real-valued functions on $X$ need not be a measurable function (unless the $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal M$ is very special).

**11.** Let $f$ be a function on $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^{k}$ such that, for each $x\in\mathbb R$, $f\left(x,\cdot\right)$ is Borel measurable, and for each $y\in\mathbb R^{k}$, $f\left(\cdot,y\right)$ is continuous. For $n\in\mathbb N$, set $a_{i}=i/n$, and define: when $a_{i}\le x\le a_{i+1}$,

$$
f_{n}\left(x,y\right)=\frac{f\left(a_{i+1},y\right)\left(x-a_{i}\right)-f\left(a_{i},y\right)\left(x-a_{i+1}\right)}{a_{i+1}-a_{i}}.
$$

Prove that $f_{n}$ is Borel measurable on $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^{k}$ and that $f_{n}\to f$ pointwise, so that $f$ is Borel measurable on $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^{k}$. Then use induction to prove: every function on $\mathbb R^{n}$ that is continuous in each variable is Borel measurable.

### Solution to Problem 1

#### 3

For $m,n\in\mathbb N$, by **Theorem 7.1.8**(1), the difference $f_{m}-f_{n}$ is measurable. By **Definition 7.1.1**, the preimage of the open interval $\left(-\frac{1}{k},\frac{1}{k}\right)$ is measurable:

$$
E_{k,m,n}:=\left\{x:\left|f_{m}\left(x\right)-f_{n}\left(x\right)\right|<\frac{1}{k}\right\}=\left(f_{m}-f_{n}\right)^{-1}\left(\left(-\frac{1}{k},\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)\in\mathcal M.
$$

By the completeness of the real numbers, $\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}\left(x\right)$ exists if and only if $\left\{f_{n}\left(x\right)\right\}$ is a Cauchy sequence, i.e. for each $k\in\mathbb N$ there exists $N$ such that for all $m,n\ge N$, $\left|f_{m}\left(x\right)-f_{n}\left(x\right)\right|<\frac{1}{k}$. Hence

$$
\left\{x:\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}\left(x\right)\text{存在}\right\}=\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{N=1}^{\infty}\bigcap_{m,n\ge N}E_{k,m,n}.
$$

Since $\mathcal M$ is closed under countable intersections and countable unions (**Definition 4.1.3**), and $\bigcap_{m,n\ge N}E_{k,m,n}$ is a countable intersection, the above set is measurable. $\blacksquare$

#### 6

Take $X=\mathbb R$ equipped with the Lebesgue $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal L$. Let $A\subset\mathbb R$ be a Lebesgue non-measurable set (Basic Knowledge, Vitali set). For each $a\in A$, define $f_{a}:=\chi_{\left\{a\right\}}$ as the indicator function of a singleton. Since the singleton $\left\{a\right\}$ is a closed set and hence a Borel set, $f_{a}$ is measurable. But the supremum of the uncountable family $\left\{f_{a}:a\in A\right\}$ satisfies

$$
\sup_{a\in A}f_{a}\left(x\right)=\begin{cases}1,&x\in A,\\0,&x\notin A,\end{cases}
$$

i.e. $\sup_{a\in A}f_{a}=\chi_{A}$. By **Example 7.3.5**, $\chi_{A}$ is measurable if and only if $A\in\mathcal L$, contradicting the non-measurability of $A$. Hence the supremum of uncountably many measurable real-valued functions need not be a measurable function. $\blacksquare$

#### 11

Fix $n$, set $c_{i}:=a_{i+1}-a_{i}=\frac{1}{n}$, and take the half-open intervals $\Delta_{i}:=\left[a_{i},a_{i+1}\right)$ ($i\in\mathbb Z$). Since the given interpolation formula takes the value $f\left(a_{i+1},y\right)$ on both pieces at the endpoint $x=a_{i+1}$, it can be represented unambiguously using half-open intervals. On $\Delta_{i}$,

$$
f_{n}\left(x,y\right)=\frac{x-a_{i}}{c_{i}}f\left(a_{i+1},y\right)+\frac{a_{i+1}-x}{c_{i}}f\left(a_{i},y\right),
$$

and hence

$$
f_{n}\left(x,y\right)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb Z}\left[\frac{x-a_{i}}{c_{i}}\chi_{\Delta_{i}}\left(x\right)f\left(a_{i+1},y\right)+\frac{a_{i+1}-x}{c_{i}}\chi_{\Delta_{i}}\left(x\right)f\left(a_{i},y\right)\right].
$$

We first prove that $f_{n}$ is measurable. For each $i$, the functions $\left(x,y\right)\mapsto\frac{x-a_{i}}{c_{i}}\chi_{\Delta_{i}}\left(x\right)$ and $\left(x,y\right)\mapsto\frac{a_{i+1}-x}{c_{i}}\chi_{\Delta_{i}}\left(x\right)$ depend only on $x$; they are products of a continuous function and the indicator function of a half-open interval, hence Borel functions on $\mathbb R$. By the hypothesis, $f\left(a_{i},y\right)$ and $f\left(a_{i+1},y\right)$ are Borel functions on $\mathbb R^{k}$. By **Theorem 4.5.4** and **Definition 4.5.2**, the product $\sigma$-algebra satisfies $\mathcal B\left(\mathbb R\right)\otimes\mathcal B\left(\mathbb R^{k}\right)=\mathcal B\left(\mathbb R^{1+k}\right)$, and by **Proposition 7.1.7**, the product of a Borel function depending only on $x$ and a Borel function depending only on $y$, as a function of two variables on $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^{k}$, is measurable. Then by **Theorem 7.1.8**(1) (finite linear combinations), $f_{n}$ is a Borel function on $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^{k}$.

We next prove that $f_{n}\to f$ pointwise. Fix $\left(x,y\right)\in\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^{k}$. Since $f\left(\cdot,y\right)$ is continuous, as $n\to\infty$ the grid $a_{i}=i/n$ refines so that $a_{i}\to x$ and $a_{i+1}\to x$, hence $f\left(a_{i},y\right)\to f\left(x,y\right)$ and $f\left(a_{i+1},y\right)\to f\left(x,y\right)$; while the interpolation coefficients $\frac{x-a_{i}}{c_{i}}$, $\frac{a_{i+1}-x}{c_{i}}\in\left[0,1\right]$ are bounded. Hence $f_{n}\left(x,y\right)\to f\left(x,y\right)$.

By **Theorem 7.1.8**(4) (the pointwise limit of measurable functions is measurable), $f=\lim_{n}f_{n}$ is Borel measurable on $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^{k}$.

Finally, we prove by induction that functions on $\mathbb R^{n}$ that are continuous in each variable are Borel measurable. For $n=1$, a continuous function pulls open sets back to open sets, hence is measurable by **Definition 4.3.2** and **Definition 7.1.1** (also by **Theorem 7.1.9**). Assume the result holds for $n-1$. For a function $f$ on $\mathbb R^{n}=\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^{n-1}$ that is continuous in each variable, for each $x$, $f\left(x,\cdot\right)$ is a function on $\mathbb R^{n-1}$ continuous in each variable and hence, by the induction hypothesis, Borel measurable; for each $y$, $f\left(\cdot,y\right)$ is continuous. By the result proved above, $f$ is Borel measurable on $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^{n-1}$, i.e. $f$ is Borel measurable on $\mathbb R^{n}$. $\blacksquare$

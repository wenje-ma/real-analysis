> **Definition 5.1.2 (Measure)**
> 
> Let $\left(X,\mathcal F\right)$ be a measurable space. A function $\mu:\mathcal F\to\left[0,+\infty\right]$ is called a measure on $\left(X,\mathcal F\right)$ if: (1) nonnegativity: $\mu\left(E\right)\ge0$ for all $E\in\mathcal F$; (2) empty set: $\mu\left(\emptyset\right)=0$; (3) countable additivity ($\sigma$-additivity): for any countable pairwise-disjoint $\left\{E_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal F$, $\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu\left(E_{n}\right)$.

> **Definition 6.2.1 (Premeasure)**
> 
> Let $X$ be nonempty and $\mathcal E\subset\mathcal P\left(X\right)$ an algebra. A function $\mu_{0}:\mathcal E\to\left[0,+\infty\right]$ is called a premeasure if: (1) $\mu_{0}\left(\varnothing\right)=0$; (2) if $\left\{E_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal E$ are pairwise disjoint and $\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\in\mathcal E$, then $\mu_{0}\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{0}\left(E_{n}\right)$.

> **Theorem 6.3.3 (Carathéodory's Theorem)**
> 
> Let $\mu^{*}$ be an outer measure on $X$. Then: (1) $\mathcal M$ is a $\sigma$-algebra; (2) $\mu^{*}|_{\mathcal M}$ is a complete measure; (3) if $\mu^{*}$ is generated by a premeasure $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{0}$ is $\sigma$-finite, then $\mu^{*}|_{\mathcal M}$ is the unique extension of $\mu_{0}$.

> **Proposition 7.1.5 (Composition Preserves Measurability)**
> 
> If $f:X\to Y$ is $\left(\mathcal F,\mathcal G\right)$-measurable and $g:Y\to Z$ is $\left(\mathcal G,\mathcal H\right)$-measurable, then $g\circ f:X\to Z$ is $\left(\mathcal F,\mathcal H\right)$-measurable.

> **Proposition 7.1.7 (Measurability on Product Spaces)**
> 
> Let $f=\left(f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\right):X\to Y_{1}\times\cdots\times Y_{n}$. With the product $\sigma$-algebra on the product space, $f$ is measurable iff each coordinate map $f_{i}:X\to Y_{i}$ is measurable.

> **Theorem 8.2.9 (Dominated Convergence Theorem)**
> 
> Let $\left(X,\mathcal F,\mu\right)$ be a measure space and $\left\{f_{n}\right\}$ a sequence of measurable functions. If there exists an integrable $g$ with $\left|f_{n}\right|\le g$ for all $n$ and $f_{n}\to f$ a.e., then $f$ is integrable and $\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu$.

> **Theorem 8.2.16 (Integrable Simple Functions Are Dense in $L^{1}$)**
> 
> If $f\in L^{1}\left(\mu\right)$ and $\varepsilon> 0$, then there exists an integrable simple function $\varphi=\sum a_{j}\chi_{E_{j}}$ with $\int\left|f-\varphi\right|\mathrm{d}\mu< \varepsilon$.

> **Definition 10.1.1 (Product $\sigma$-Algebra)**
> 
> Let $\left(X,\mathcal A,\mu\right)$ and $\left(Y,\mathcal B,\nu\right)$ be two measure spaces. The product $\sigma$-algebra on $X\times Y$ is $\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B=\sigma\left(\left\{A\times B:A\in\mathcal A,B\in\mathcal B\right\}\right)$, i.e. the $\sigma$-algebra generated by all measurable rectangles.

> **Definition 10.1.2 (Sections)**
> 
> For $E\subseteq X\times Y$, define its x-section $E_{x}=\left\{y\in Y:\left(x,y\right)\in E\right\}$ and y-section $E^{y}=\left\{x\in X:\left(x,y\right)\in E\right\}$.

> **Theorem 10.1.4 (Existence and Uniqueness of the Product Measure)**
> 
> Let $\left(X,\mathcal A,\mu\right)$ and $\left(Y,\mathcal B,\nu\right)$ be $\sigma$-finite measure spaces. There is a unique measure $\mu\times\nu$ on $\left(X\times Y,\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B\right)$ such that $\left(\mu\times\nu\right)\left(A\times B\right)=\mu\left(A\right)\nu\left(B\right)$ for all $A\in\mathcal A$, $B\in\mathcal B$. It is constructed as $\phi\left(E\right)=\int_{X}\nu\left(E_{x}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)$ for $E\in\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B$.

> **Theorem 10.2.1 (Fubini's Theorem)**
> 
> Let $\left(X,\mathcal A,\mu\right)$ and $\left(Y,\mathcal B,\nu\right)$ be $\sigma$-finite measure spaces and $f:X\times Y\to\mathbb R$ be $\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B$-measurable. If $f$ is integrable on $X\times Y$, then for almost every $x$, $y\mapsto f\left(x,y\right)$ is $\nu$-integrable; for almost every $y$, $x\mapsto f\left(x,y\right)$ is $\mu$-integrable; and the iterated integrals agree:

$$
\int_{X\times Y}f\,\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)=\int_{X}\left(\int_{Y}f\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(y\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)=\int_{Y}\left(\int_{X}f\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(y\right).
$$

> **Theorem 10.2.2 (Tonelli's Theorem)**
> 
> If $f:X\times Y\to\left[0,\infty\right]$ is $\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B$-measurable, then

$$
\int_{X\times Y}f\,\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)=\int_{X}\left(\int_{Y}f\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(y\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)=\int_{Y}\left(\int_{X}f\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(y\right),
$$
with all integral values in $\left[0,\infty\right]$.

> **Theorem 11.1.1 (Regularity of Lebesgue Measure and Rectangle Approximation)**
> 
> Let $E\in\mathcal L^{n}$. (a)

$$
m\left(E\right)=\inf\left\{m\left(U\right):U\supset E,U\text{ open}\right\}=\sup\left\{m\left(K\right):K\subset E,K\text{ compact}\right\};
$$
(b) if $m\left(E\right)< \infty$, then for every $\varepsilon> 0$ there is a finite family of pairwise-disjoint rectangles $\left\{R_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{N}$ with interval sides such that $m\left(E\triangle\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}R_{j}\right)< \varepsilon$.

> **Theorem 11.4.1 (Integration in Polar Coordinates)**
> 
> There is a unique Borel measure $\sigma=\sigma_{n-1}$ on $S^{n-1}$ such that $m^{*}=\rho\times\sigma$. If $f$ is Borel measurable on $\mathbb R^{n}$ and either $f\ge0$ or $f\in L^{1}\left(m\right)$, then

$$
\int_{\mathbb R^{n}}f\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{S^{n-1}}f\left(rx'\right)r^{n-1}\mathrm{d}\sigma\left(x'\right)\mathrm{d}r.
$$

## 1

Let $X=Y=\mathbb N$, $\mathcal M=\mathcal N=\mathcal P\left(\mathbb N\right)$, and $\mu=\nu$ be counting measure. Define $f\left(m,n\right)=1$ if $m=n$, $f\left(m,n\right)=-1$ if $m=n+1$, and $f\left(m,n\right)=0$ otherwise. Then $\int\left|f\right|\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)=\infty$, and $\iint f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\nu$ and $\iint f\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,\mathrm{d}\mu$ exist but are not equal.

### Solution to 1

Compute $\int\left|f\right|\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)=\sum_{m,n}\left|f\left(m,n\right)\right|$. $f$ takes value $1$ on the diagonal $\left\{\left(m,m\right)\right\}$ and $-1$ on the subdiagonal $\left\{\left(m+1,m\right)\right\}$, so $\left|f\right|$ takes value $1$ at infinitely many points in these two families; hence $\int\left|f\right|\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)=\sum_{m,n}\left|f\left(m,n\right)\right|=\infty$.

Compute $\iint f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\nu$ (inner summation over $\mu$, outer over $\nu$). For fixed $n$, $\sum_{m}f\left(m,n\right)=f\left(n,n\right)+f\left(n+1,n\right)=1+\left(-1\right)=0$, so $\iint f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\nu=\sum_{n}0=0$.

Compute $\iint f\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,\mathrm{d}\mu$ (inner over $\nu$). For fixed $m$, when $m\ge1$, $\sum_{n}f\left(m,n\right)=f\left(m,m\right)+f\left(m,m-1\right)=1+\left(-1\right)=0$; while for $m=0$, $f\left(0,n\right)$ is $1$ only at $n=0$, so the sum is $1$. Hence $\iint f\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\sum_{m}\left(\sum_{n}f\left(m,n\right)\right)=1$.

Thus $\iint f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\nu=0\ne1=\iint f\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,\mathrm{d}\mu$. Since $\int\left|f\right|\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)=\infty$, $f$ is not absolutely integrable, the hypothesis $\int\left|f\right|\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)< \infty$ of **Theorem 10.2.1 (Fubini's Theorem)** fails, and the order of integration cannot be exchanged. $\blacksquare$

## 2

Let $\left(X,\mathcal M,\mu\right)$ be a $\sigma$-finite measure space and $f\in L^{+}\left(X\right)$. Set $G_{f}=\left\{\left(x,y\right)\in X\times\left[0,\infty\right]:y\le f\left(x\right)\right\}$. Then $G_{f}$ is $\mathcal M\otimes\mathcal B_{\mathbb R}$-measurable and $\mu\times m\left(G_{f}\right)=\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu$; if the inequality $y\le f\left(x\right)$ is replaced by $y< f\left(x\right)$, the conclusion still holds.

### Solution to 2

Measurability. Consider the map $T:X\times\left[0,\infty\right]\to\left[0,\infty\right]\times\left[0,\infty\right]$, $T\left(x,y\right)=\left(f\left(x\right),y\right)$. By **Proposition 7.1.7 (Measurability on Product Spaces)**, $T$ is measurable iff its coordinate functions are measurable; the coordinate $\left(x,y\right)\mapsto f\left(x\right)=f\circ p_{1}$ is measurable (since $f$ is measurable), and $\left(x,y\right)\mapsto y=p_{2}$ is continuous hence measurable. Thus $T$ is measurable. Also $\Phi\left(z,y\right)=z-y$ is continuous, hence Borel measurable. By **Proposition 7.1.5 (Composition Preserves Measurability)**, $\left(x,y\right)\mapsto f\left(x\right)-y=\Phi\circ T$ is measurable. Since

$$
G_{f}=\left\{\left(x,y\right):f\left(x\right)-y\ge0\right\}=\left(f-y\right)^{-1}\left(\left[0,\infty\right]\right),
$$

a preimage of a measurable function, by **Definition 10.1.1 (Product $\sigma$-Algebra)**, $G_{f}\in\mathcal M\otimes\mathcal B_{\mathbb R}$.

Measure value. By **Theorem 10.2.2 (Tonelli's Theorem)** (nonnegative characteristic function, $\mu\times m$ is $\sigma$-finite),

$$
\left(\mu\times m\right)\left(G_{f}\right)=\int_{X}m\left(\left(G_{f}\right)_{x}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right),
$$

where $\left(G_{f}\right)_{x}=\left\{y\in\left[0,\infty\right]:y\le f\left(x\right)\right\}=\left[0,f\left(x\right)\right]$, and Lebesgue measure $m\left(\left[0,f\left(x\right)\right]\right)=f\left(x\right)$. Hence $\left(\mu\times m\right)\left(G_{f}\right)=\int_{X}f\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)$.

If $y\le f\left(x\right)$ is replaced by $y< f\left(x\right)$, then $\left(G_{f}\right)_{x}=\left[0,f\left(x\right)\right)$, still with $m=f\left(x\right)$, and the conclusion is unchanged. $\blacksquare$

## 3

If $f\in L^{1}\left(m\right)$ and $\varepsilon> 0$, then there is a simple function $\varphi=\sum a_{j}\chi_{R_{j}}$, each $R_{j}$ a product of intervals, with $\int\left|f-\varphi\right|< \varepsilon$; and there is a continuous function $g$ vanishing outside a bounded set with $\int\left|f-g\right|< \varepsilon$.

### Solution to 3

Simple-function part. By **Theorem 8.2.16 (Integrable Simple Functions Are Dense in $L^{1}$)**, there is an integrable simple function $\varphi=\sum_{j}a_{j}\chi_{E_{j}}$ with $\int\left|f-\varphi\right|< \varepsilon/2$. For the terms with $a_{j}\ne0$, since $\int a_{j}\chi_{E_{j}}\,\mathrm{d}m=a_{j}m\left(E_{j}\right)< \infty$, we have $m\left(E_{j}\right)< \infty$ (otherwise the integral diverges, contradicting integrability). By **Theorem 11.1.1 (Regularity of Lebesgue Measure and Rectangle Approximation)** (b), for each such $E_{j}$ there are finitely many pairwise-disjoint rectangles $\left\{R_{j,k}\right\}$ with interval sides such that $m\left(E_{j}\triangle\bigcup_{k}R_{j,k}\right)$ is arbitrarily small. Thus $\chi_{E_{j}}$ and $\chi_{R_{j}}$ (with $R_{j}=\bigcup_{k}R_{j,k}$) are arbitrarily close in $L^{1}$; taking the approximation errors small enough and combining over all $j$, we obtain a simple function $\varphi'=\sum_{j}a_{j}\chi_{R_{j}}$ with $\int\left|\varphi-\varphi'\right|< \varepsilon/2$. Hence $\int\left|f-\varphi'\right|\le\int\left|f-\varphi\right|+\int\left|\varphi-\varphi'\right|< \varepsilon$.

Continuous-function part. Let $\varphi'=\sum_{j}a_{j}\chi_{R_{j}}$ be as obtained above, each $R_{j}$ a bounded closed rectangle. By Continuous Approximation of Characteristic Functions, for each $R_{j}$ there is a continuous compactly supported $g_{j}$ with $\int\left|\chi_{R_{j}}-g_{j}\right|\mathrm{d}m$ arbitrarily small. Taking the errors small enough and setting $g=\sum_{j}a_{j}g_{j}$, $g$ is continuous, supported in $\bigcup_{j}R_{j}$ (bounded), and $\int\left|\varphi'-g\right|\le\sum_{j}\left|a_{j}\right|\int\left|\chi_{R_{j}}-g_{j}\right|< \varepsilon/2$. Hence $\int\left|f-g\right|\le\int\left|f-\varphi'\right|+\int\left|\varphi'-g\right|< \varepsilon$, and $g$ vanishes outside a bounded set. $\blacksquare$

## 4

(Linear change of variables) Assume $T\in GL\left(n,\mathbb R\right)$.

(a) If $f$ is Lebesgue measurable on $\mathbb R^{n}$, then $f\circ T$ is also. If $f\ge0$ or $f\in L^{1}\left(m\right)$, then $\int f\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x=\left|\det T\right|\int f\circ T\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x$.

(b) If $E\in\mathcal L^{n}$, then $T\left(E\right)\in\mathcal L^{n}$ and $m\left(T\left(E\right)\right)=\left|\det T\right|m\left(E\right)$.

### Solution to 4

First treat the three elementary transformations, then combine.

Dilation $T_{1}\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{j},\dots,x_{n}\right)=\left(x_{1},\dots,cx_{j},\dots,x_{n}\right)$ ($c\ne0$). By Invariance Under Translation and Dilation in One Dimension and the product structure, dilating one coordinate while fixing the others gives $m\left(T_{1}\left(E\right)\right)=\left|c\right|m\left(E\right)$ and $\int f\circ T_{1}\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{\left|c\right|}\int f\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x$, i.e. $\int f\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x=\left|\det T_{1}\right|\int f\circ T_{1}\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x$.

Shear $T_{2}\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{j},\dots,x_{n}\right)=\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{j}+cx_{k},\dots,x_{n}\right)$ ($k\ne j$). By Invariance Under Translation and Dilation in One Dimension applied slicewise, $m\left(T_{2}\left(E\right)\right)=m\left(E\right)$ and $\int f\circ T_{2}\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x=\int f\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x$. Since $\det T_{2}=1$, the formula holds.

Swap $T_{3}$ swaps two coordinates, $\det T_{3}=-1$. By the symmetry of $m$ as a product measure, $m\left(T_{3}\left(E\right)\right)=m\left(E\right)$ and $\int f\circ T_{3}\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x=\int f\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x$, and $\left|\det T_{3}\right|=1$, so the formula holds.

General case. By elementary linear algebra, each $T\in GL\left(n,\mathbb R\right)$ is a finite product $T=T_{1}\circ\cdots\circ T_{k}$ of the three elementary transformations above (an invertible matrix reduces to the identity by row operations). For each elementary transform the above holds, and determinants multiply: $\left|\det T\right|=\prod_{i}\left|\det T_{i}\right|$. Substituting successively,

$$
\int f\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x=\left|\det T_{1}\right|\int f\circ T_{1}\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x=\cdots=\left|\det T\right|\int f\circ T\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x,
$$

which is (a). Measurability: $T$ is continuous, and by Invertible Linear Maps Preserve Measurability and Null Sets, $f\circ T$ is measurable and $T\left(E\right)\in\mathcal L^{n}$.

Proof of (b). Take $f=\chi_{T\left(E\right)}$ in (a):

$$
m\left(T\left(E\right)\right)=\int\chi_{T\left(E\right)}\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x=\left|\det T\right|\int\chi_{T\left(E\right)}\circ T\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x=\left|\det T\right|\int\chi_{E}\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x=\left|\det T\right|m\left(E\right).
$$

$T\left(E\right)\in\mathcal L^{n}$ follows from measurability. $\blacksquare$

## 5

Let $E=\left[0,1\right]\times\left[0,1\right]$. Study the existence and equality of $\int_{E}f\,\mathrm{d}m^{2}$, $\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y$, and $\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x$, for the following $f$.

(a) $f\left(x,y\right)=\left(x^{2}-y^{2}\right)\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)^{-2}$;

(b) $f\left(x,y\right)=\left(1-xy\right)^{-a}$ ($a> 0$);

(c) $f\left(x,y\right)=\left(x-y\right)^{-3}$ if $0< y< x< 1$, and $0$ otherwise.

### Solution to 5

(a). $\left|f\left(x,y\right)\right|=\frac{\left|x^{2}-y^{2}\right|}{\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)^{2}}\le\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{x^{2}+y^{2}}$. On $E$, $\int_{E}\frac{1}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y$ diverges (in polar coordinates, $\int_{0}^{\delta}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{1}{r^{2}}r\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}r=\frac{\pi}{2}\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{1}{r}\mathrm{d}r=\infty$), so $\int_{E}\left|f\right|=\infty$, $f\notin L^{1}$, and $\int_{E}f\,\mathrm{d}m^{2}$ is undefined.

First integrating in $x$. Fix $y$, set $x=yt$; then $\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x^{2}-y^{2}}{\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)^{2}}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{y}\int_{0}^{1/y}\frac{t^{2}-1}{\left(t^{2}+1\right)^{2}}\mathrm{d}t$. Since $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\frac{t}{t^{2}+1}\right)=\frac{1-t^{2}}{\left(t^{2}+1\right)^{2}}$, we have $\int\frac{t^{2}-1}{\left(t^{2}+1\right)^{2}}\mathrm{d}t=-\frac{t}{t^{2}+1}+C$, so the inner integral $=\frac{1}{y}\left[-\frac{t}{t^{2}+1}\right]_{0}^{1/y}=-\frac{1}{y^{2}+1}$. Hence $\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{0}^{1}-\frac{1}{1+y^{2}}\mathrm{d}y=-\left[\arctan y\right]_{0}^{1}=-\frac{\pi}{4}$.

First integrating in $y$. Symmetrically, fix $x$, set $y=xt$; the inner integral $\int_{0}^{1}f\,\mathrm{d}y=\frac{1}{x^{2}+1}$, so $\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\pi}{4}$.

Conclusion: $\int_{E}f\,\mathrm{d}m^{2}$ does not exist; $\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y=-\frac{\pi}{4}$ and $\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\pi}{4}$ exist but are unequal and opposite in sign. Since $f\notin L^{1}$, **Theorem 10.2.1 (Fubini's Theorem)** does not apply, and the iterated integrals cannot be exchanged.

(b). $f\ge0$. Fix $y$; the inner integral $\int_{0}^{1}\left(1-xy\right)^{-a}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{y}\cdot\frac{1-\left(1-y\right)^{1-a}}{1-a}$ (when $a\ne1$), and for $a=1$ the inner integral $=-\frac{\ln\left(1-y\right)}{y}$. For $0< a< 1$, the inner integral tends to $1$ as $y\to0$ and to $\frac{1}{1-a}$ as $y\to1$ (since $\left(1-y\right)^{1-a}\to0$), hence is bounded, and $\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y< \infty$. For $a=1$, $\int_{0}^{1}-\frac{\ln\left(1-y\right)}{y}\mathrm{d}y=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{2}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}< \infty$. Thus for $0< a\le1$, $f\in L^{1}$, and by **Theorem 10.2.2 (Tonelli's Theorem)**, all three integrals exist and agree.

For $a> 1$, the inner integral $\frac{1}{y}\cdot\frac{1-\left(1-y\right)^{1-a}}{1-a}$; since $1-a< 0$ and $\left(1-y\right)^{1-a}\to\infty$ as $y\to1$, the inner integral tends to $+\infty$, so $\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y=+\infty$; symmetrically $\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x=+\infty$, and $\int_{E}f\,\mathrm{d}m^{2}=+\infty$.

Conclusion: for $0< a\le1$, the three integrals exist and agree, $f\in L^{1}$; for $a> 1$, all three are $+\infty$, $f\notin L^{1}$.

(c). The region is $\left\{\left(x,y\right):0< y< x< 1\right\}$. $\left|f\right|=\left(x-y\right)^{-3}$. First integrating in $x$, $\int_{y}^{1}\left(x-y\right)^{-3}\mathrm{d}x=\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(x-y\right)^{-2}\right]_{y}^{1}=+\infty$, so $\int_{E}\left|f\right|=\infty$, $f\notin L^{1}$, and $\int_{E}f\,\mathrm{d}m^{2}$ is undefined.

First integrating in $x$: fix $y$, $\int_{y}^{1}\left(x-y\right)^{-3}\mathrm{d}x=\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(x-y\right)^{-2}\right]_{y}^{1}$; since $\lim_{x\to y^{+}}\left(x-y\right)^{-2}=\infty$, the inner integral $=+\infty$, so $\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y=+\infty$.

First integrating in $y$: fix $x$, $\int_{0}^{x}\left(x-y\right)^{-3}\mathrm{d}y=\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(x-y\right)^{-2}\right]_{0}^{x}$; since $\lim_{y\to x^{-}}\left(x-y\right)^{-2}=\infty$, the inner integral $=-\infty$, so $\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x=-\infty$.

Conclusion: $\int_{E}f\,\mathrm{d}m^{2}$ does not exist; $\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y=+\infty$ while $\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x=-\infty$. This is the classic counterexample where Fubini fails: without absolute integrability the order of integration cannot be exchanged. $\blacksquare$

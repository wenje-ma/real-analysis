> **Definition 4.1.3 ($\sigma$-Algebra)**
> 
> Let $X$ be nonempty and $\mathcal F\subset\mathcal P\left(X\right)$. $\mathcal F$ is called a $\sigma$-algebra on $X$ if: (1) $X\in\mathcal F$; (2) closed under complements: $A\in\mathcal F\Rightarrow A^{c}\in\mathcal F$; (3) closed under countable unions: $\left\{A_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal F\Rightarrow\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\in\mathcal F$.

> **Definition 5.1.2 (Measure)**
> 
> Let $\left(X,\mathcal F\right)$ be a measurable space. A function $\mu:\mathcal F\to\left[0,+\infty\right]$ is called a measure on $\left(X,\mathcal F\right)$ if: (1) nonnegativity: $\mu\left(E\right)\ge0$ for all $E\in\mathcal F$; (2) empty set: $\mu\left(\emptyset\right)=0$; (3) countable additivity ($\sigma$-additivity): for any countable pairwise-disjoint $\left\{E_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal F$, $\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu\left(E_{n}\right)$.

> **Definition 5.1.5 ($\sigma$-Finite Measure)**
> 
> Let $\left(X,\mathcal F,\mu\right)$ be a measure space. If there exists a sequence of measurable sets $\left\{E_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal F$ such that $X=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}$ and $\mu\left(E_{n}\right)< +\infty$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$, then $\mu$ is called $\sigma$-finite.

> **Definition 6.2.1 (Premeasure)**
> 
> Let $X$ be nonempty and $\mathcal E\subset\mathcal P\left(X\right)$ an algebra. A function $\mu_{0}:\mathcal E\to\left[0,+\infty\right]$ is called a premeasure if: (1) $\mu_{0}\left(\varnothing\right)=0$; (2) if $\left\{E_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal E$ are pairwise disjoint and $\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\in\mathcal E$, then $\mu_{0}\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{0}\left(E_{n}\right)$.

> **Theorem 6.3.3 (Carathéodory's Theorem)**
> 
> Let $\mu^{*}$ be an outer measure on $X$. Then: (1) $\mathcal M$ is a $\sigma$-algebra; (2) $\mu^{*}|_{\mathcal M}$ is a complete measure; (3) if $\mu^{*}$ is generated by a premeasure $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{0}$ is $\sigma$-finite, then $\mu^{*}|_{\mathcal M}$ is the unique extension of $\mu_{0}$.

> **Proposition 7.1.5 (Composition Preserves Measurability)**
> 
> If $f:X\to Y$ is $\left(\mathcal F,\mathcal G\right)$-measurable and $g:Y\to Z$ is $\left(\mathcal G,\mathcal H\right)$-measurable, then $g\circ f:X\to Z$ is $\left(\mathcal F,\mathcal H\right)$-measurable.

> **Proposition 7.1.7 (Measurability on Product Spaces)**
> 
> Let $f=\left(f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\right):X\to Y_{1}\times\cdots\times Y_{n}$. With the product $\sigma$-algebra on the product space, $f$ is measurable iff each coordinate map $f_{i}:X\to Y_{i}$ is measurable.

> **Theorem 7.1.8 (Closure of Real-Valued Measurable Functions Under Operations)**
> 
> Let $f,g:X\to\mathbb R$ be measurable and $c\in\mathbb R$. Then $cf$, $f+g$, $fg$, $\max\left(f,g\right)$, and $\min\left(f,g\right)$ are measurable; if $\left\{f_{n}\right\}$ are measurable and converge pointwise, the limit function is measurable.

> **Theorem 7.2.2 (Construction of Measurable Functions)**
> 
> If $f:X\to\left[0,+\infty\right]$ is a nonnegative measurable function, then there exists a sequence of nonnegative simple functions $\left\{f_{n}\right\}$ with $0\le f_{1}\le f_{2}\le\cdots\le f$ and $\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}\left(x\right)=f\left(x\right)$ for every $x\in X$.

> **Definition 9.1.3 (Convergence in Measure)**
> 
> Let $\left\{f_{n}\right\}$ be a sequence of measurable functions and $f$ measurable. If for every $\varepsilon> 0$, $\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu\left(\left\{x:\left|f_{n}\left(x\right)-f\left(x\right)\right|> \varepsilon\right\}\right)=0$, then $\left\{f_{n}\right\}$ converges to $f$ in measure, written $f_{n}\xrightarrow{\mu}f$.

> **Theorem 9.1.8 (Subsequence Property)**
> 
> If $f_{n}\xrightarrow{\mu}f$, then there exists a subsequence $\left\{f_{n_{k}}\right\}$ such that $f_{n_{k}}\to f$ almost everywhere.

> **Theorem 9.1.15 (Egorov's Theorem)**
> 
> Let $\mu\left(X\right)< \infty$ and $f_{n}\to f$ almost everywhere. Then for every $\delta> 0$ there exists a measurable set $E\subset X$ with $\mu\left(E\right)< \delta$ such that $f_{n}$ converges uniformly to $f$ on $X\setminus E$. In particular, on a finite-measure space, almost-everywhere convergence implies convergence in measure.

> **Definition 10.1.1 (Product $\sigma$-Algebra)**
> 
> Let $\left(X,\mathcal A,\mu\right)$ and $\left(Y,\mathcal B,\nu\right)$ be two measure spaces. The product $\sigma$-algebra on $X\times Y$ is $\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B=\sigma\left(\left\{A\times B:A\in\mathcal A,B\in\mathcal B\right\}\right)$, i.e. the $\sigma$-algebra generated by all measurable rectangles.

> **Definition 10.1.2 (Sections)**
> 
> For $E\subseteq X\times Y$, define its x-section $E_{x}=\left\{y\in Y:\left(x,y\right)\in E\right\}$ and y-section $E^{y}=\left\{x\in X:\left(x,y\right)\in E\right\}$.

> **Theorem 10.1.4 (Existence and Uniqueness of the Product Measure)**
> 
> Let $\left(X,\mathcal A,\mu\right)$ and $\left(Y,\mathcal B,\nu\right)$ be $\sigma$-finite measure spaces. There is a unique measure $\mu\times\nu$ on $\left(X\times Y,\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B\right)$ such that $\left(\mu\times\nu\right)\left(A\times B\right)=\mu\left(A\right)\nu\left(B\right)$ for all $A\in\mathcal A$, $B\in\mathcal B$. It is constructed as $\phi\left(E\right)=\int_{X}\nu\left(E_{x}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)$ for $E\in\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B$.

> **Theorem 10.2.1 (Fubini's Theorem)**
> 
> Let $\left(X,\mathcal A,\mu\right)$ and $\left(Y,\mathcal B,\nu\right)$ be $\sigma$-finite measure spaces and $f:X\times Y\to\mathbb R$ be $\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B$-measurable. If $f$ is integrable on $X\times Y$, then for almost every $x$, $y\mapsto f\left(x,y\right)$ is $\nu$-integrable; for almost every $y$, $x\mapsto f\left(x,y\right)$ is $\mu$-integrable; and the iterated integrals agree:

$$
\int_{X\times Y}f\,\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)=\int_{X}\left(\int_{Y}f\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(y\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)=\int_{Y}\left(\int_{X}f\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(y\right).
$$

> **Theorem 10.2.2 (Tonelli's Theorem)**
> 
> If $f:X\times Y\to\left[0,\infty\right]$ is $\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B$-measurable, then

$$
\int_{X\times Y}f\,\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)=\int_{X}\left(\int_{Y}f\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(y\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)=\int_{Y}\left(\int_{X}f\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(y\right),
$$
with all integral values in $\left[0,\infty\right]$.

## 1

Let $\mu\left(X\right)< \infty$. If $f,g$ are complex-valued measurable functions on $X$, define $\rho\left(f,g\right)=\int\frac{\left|f-g\right|}{1+\left|f-g\right|}\,\mathrm{d}\mu$. Then $\rho$ is a metric on the space of measurable functions (identifying functions equal a.e.), and $f_{n}\to f$ in this metric iff $f_{n}\to f$ in measure.

### Solution to 1

Write $d\left(t\right)=\frac{t}{1+t}$ ($t\ge0$). By Properties of the Map $t\mapsto\frac{t}{1+t}$, $d$ is increasing and subadditive. The integrand $\frac{\left|f-g\right|}{1+\left|f-g\right|}=d\left(\left|f-g\right|\right)$ is nonnegative and bounded ($\le1$), measurable, hence integrable, so $\rho$ is well-defined.

- $\rho\left(f,g\right)\ge0$; $\rho\left(f,g\right)=0$ iff $d\left(\left|f-g\right|\right)=0$ a.e. (by Nonnegative Integrable Function with Zero Integral Vanishes Almost Everywhere), iff $\left|f-g\right|=0$ a.e., iff $f=g$ a.e. Thus on the quotient space (identifying functions equal a.e.), $\rho\left(f,g\right)=0$ iff $f=g$.
- Symmetry is clear: $\rho\left(f,g\right)=\rho\left(g,f\right)$.
- Triangle inequality: by $\left|f-g\right|\le\left|f-h\right|+\left|h-g\right|$ and the increase and subadditivity of $d$,

$$
d\left(\left|f-g\right|\right)\le d\left(\left|f-h\right|+\left|h-g\right|\right)\le d\left(\left|f-h\right|\right)+d\left(\left|h-g\right|\right),
$$

and integrating gives $\rho\left(f,g\right)\le\rho\left(f,h\right)+\rho\left(h,g\right)$.

Thus $\rho$ is a metric on the quotient space.

Now prove equivalence of convergence. If $f_{n}\xrightarrow{\mu}f$, for $\varepsilon\in\left(0,1\right)$,

$$
\rho\left(f_{n},f\right)=\int_{\left\{\left|f_{n}-f\right|\le\varepsilon\right\}}d\left(\left|f_{n}-f\right|\right)\mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{\left\{\left|f_{n}-f\right|> \varepsilon\right\}}d\left(\left|f_{n}-f\right|\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\le d\left(\varepsilon\right)\mu\left(X\right)+\mu\left(\left\{\left|f_{n}-f\right|> \varepsilon\right\}\right).
$$

By **Definition 9.1.3 (Convergence in Measure)**, first let $n\to\infty$ so the second term $\to0$ (since $f_{n}\xrightarrow{\mu}f$), then let $\varepsilon\to0$ so $d\left(\varepsilon\right)=\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\to0$, giving $\rho\left(f_{n},f\right)\to0$.

Conversely, if $\rho\left(f_{n},f\right)\to0$, since $d$ is increasing, on $\left\{\left|f_{n}-f\right|> \varepsilon\right\}$ we have $d\left(\left|f_{n}-f\right|\right)\ge d\left(\varepsilon\right)> 0$, so

$$
\rho\left(f_{n},f\right)\ge\int_{\left\{\left|f_{n}-f\right|> \varepsilon\right\}}d\left(\left|f_{n}-f\right|\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\ge d\left(\varepsilon\right)\mu\left(\left\{\left|f_{n}-f\right|> \varepsilon\right\}\right),
$$

i.e.

$$
\mu\left(\left\{\left|f_{n}-f\right|> \varepsilon\right\}\right)\le\frac{\rho\left(f_{n},f\right)}{d\left(\varepsilon\right)}\to0.
$$

By **Definition 9.1.3 (Convergence in Measure)**, $f_{n}\xrightarrow{\mu}f$. $\blacksquare$

## 2

If $\mu$ is $\sigma$-finite and $f_{n}\to f$ almost everywhere, then there exist measurable sets $E_{1},E_{2},\dots\subset X$ such that $\mu\left(\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_{i}\right)^{c}\right)=0$, and $f_{n}\to f$ uniformly on each $E_{i}$.

### Solution to 2

By **Definition 5.1.5 ($\sigma$-Finite Measure)**, $\mu$ is $\sigma$-finite, so $X=\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}X_{m}$ with $\mu\left(X_{m}\right)< \infty$. Fix $m$; on $X_{m}$ we have $\mu\left(X_{m}\right)< \infty$ and $f_{n}\to f$ a.e. (**Definition 9.1.1 (Almost-Everywhere Convergence)**). By **Theorem 9.1.15 (Egorov's Theorem)**, for each $j\in\mathbb N$ there is a measurable set $E_{mj}\subset X_{m}$ with $\mu\left(X_{m}\setminus E_{mj}\right)< 2^{-j}$ and $f_{n}\to f$ uniformly on $E_{mj}$.

Let $F_{m}:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}E_{mj}$. Then $X_{m}\setminus F_{m}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(X_{m}\setminus E_{mj}\right)$, and $\mu\left(X_{m}\setminus F_{m}\right)\le\mu\left(X_{m}\setminus E_{mj}\right)< 2^{-j}$ for all $j$, so $\mu\left(X_{m}\setminus F_{m}\right)=0$. Let $F:=\bigcup_{m,j}E_{mj}=\bigcup_{m}F_{m}$; then

$$
\mu\left(X\setminus F\right)=\mu\left(\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}\left(X_{m}\setminus F_{m}\right)\right)\le\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mu\left(X_{m}\setminus F_{m}\right)=0,
$$

since $X=\bigcup_{m}X_{m}$. Hence $\mu\left(\left(\bigcup_{m,j}E_{mj}\right)^{c}\right)=0$. Reindex the countable family $\left\{E_{mj}:m,j\in\mathbb N\right\}$ as $E_{1},E_{2},\dots$; then $\mu\left(\left(\bigcup_{i}E_{i}\right)^{c}\right)=0$ and $f_{n}\to f$ uniformly on each $E_{i}$. $\blacksquare$

## 3

Let $\mu\left(X\right)< \infty$ and $f:X\times\left[0,1\right]\to\mathbb C$ satisfy: for each $y\in\left[0,1\right]$, $f\left(\cdot,y\right)$ is measurable; for each $x\in X$, $f\left(x,\cdot\right)$ is continuous.

(a) If $0< \varepsilon,\delta< 1$, then $E_{\varepsilon,\delta}=\left\{x:\left|f\left(x,y\right)-f\left(x,0\right)\right|\le\varepsilon\text{ for all }y< \delta\right\}$ is measurable;

(b) for every $\varepsilon> 0$ there exists a set $E\subset X$ with $\mu\left(E\right)< \varepsilon$ such that $f\left(\cdot,y\right)\to f\left(\cdot,0\right)$ uniformly on $E^{c}$ as $y\to0$.

### Solution to 3

(a) For each $y\in\left[0,1\right]$, by **Theorem 7.1.8 (Closure of Real-Valued Measurable Functions Under Operations)** (complex-valued split into real and imaginary parts), $f\left(\cdot,y\right)-f\left(\cdot,0\right)$ is measurable, so $\left\{x:\left|f\left(x,y\right)-f\left(x,0\right)\right|\le\varepsilon\right\}$ is measurable. Also, since $f\left(x,\cdot\right)$ is continuous, the map $y\mapsto\left|f\left(x,y\right)-f\left(x,0\right)\right|$ is continuous, so "$\left|f\left(x,y\right)-f\left(x,0\right)\right|\le\varepsilon$ for all $y\in\left[0,\delta\right)$" iff "$\le\varepsilon$ for all rational $y\in\left[0,\delta\right)$" (by density, the inequality holds at rational points iff it holds on the closure). Thus

$$
E_{\varepsilon,\delta}=\bigcap_{y\in\left[0,\delta\right)\cap\mathbb Q}\left\{x:\left|f\left(x,y\right)-f\left(x,0\right)\right|\le\varepsilon\right\},
$$

a countable intersection of measurable sets, measurable by **Definition 4.1.3 ($\sigma$-Algebra)** (closed under countable intersections). $\blacksquare$

(b) Fix $\eta> 0$ as the tolerance of uniform convergence. For each $\delta> 0$, set $F_{\delta}:=\left\{x:\left|f\left(x,y\right)-f\left(x,0\right)\right|\le\eta\text{ for all }y< \delta\right\}$, measurable by (a), and increasing as $\delta$ decreases. Since $f\left(x,\cdot\right)$ is continuous at $0$, for each $x$, $\lim_{y\to0}f\left(x,y\right)=f\left(x,0\right)$, so there exists $\delta> 0$ with $\left|f\left(x,y\right)-f\left(x,0\right)\right|\le\eta$ for all $y< \delta$, i.e. $x\in F_{\delta}$. Hence $\bigcup_{\delta> 0}F_{\delta}=X$.

By Lower Continuity of a Measure, since $\mu\left(X\right)< \infty$ and $F_{\delta}\uparrow X$, we have $\mu\left(X\setminus F_{\delta}\right)\to0$. Given $\varepsilon> 0$, choose $\delta> 0$ with $\mu\left(X\setminus F_{\delta}\right)< \varepsilon$, set $E:=X\setminus F_{\delta}$; then $\mu\left(E\right)< \varepsilon$, and on $E^{c}=F_{\delta}$, $\left|f\left(x,y\right)-f\left(x,0\right)\right|\le\eta$ for all $y< \delta$, i.e. $f\left(\cdot,y\right)\to f\left(\cdot,0\right)$ uniformly on $E^{c}$ as $y\to0$. $\blacksquare$

## 4

(Lusin's theorem) If $f:\left[a,b\right]\to\mathbb C$ is Lebesgue measurable and $\varepsilon> 0$, then there exists a compact set $E\subset\left[a,b\right]$ with $\mu\left(E^{c}\right)< \varepsilon$ such that $f|_{E}$ is continuous.

### Solution to 4

First treat the simple-function case. Let $\phi=\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}\chi_{A_{j}}$ be a simple function on $\left[a,b\right]$, with the $A_{j}$ pairwise disjoint measurable and $\bigcup_{j}A_{j}=\left[a,b\right]$. By inner regularity of Lebesgue measure (**Theorem 6.4.5 (Regularity of Lebesgue-Stieltjes Measures)**), for each $j$ choose a compact set $K_{j}\subset A_{j}$ with $m\left(A_{j}\setminus K_{j}\right)< \varepsilon/\left(2N\right)$. Set $K:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}K_{j}\subset\left[a,b\right]$; then $K$ is compact (finite union of compact sets), and

$$
m\left(\left[a,b\right]\setminus K\right)\le\sum_{j=1}^{N}m\left(A_{j}\setminus K_{j}\right)< \varepsilon/2.
$$

Since the $K_{j}$ are pairwise disjoint and $\phi$ is constant $c_{j}$ on $K_{j}$, $\phi|_{K}$ is continuous.

Now the general case. Write $f=\left(\operatorname{Re}f\right)^{+}-\left(\operatorname{Re}f\right)^{-}+i\left[\left(\operatorname{Im}f\right)^{+}-\left(\operatorname{Im}f\right)^{-}\right]$. By **Theorem 7.2.2 (Construction of Measurable Functions)**, approximate each nonnegative part pointwise by simple functions, so there exist simple $\phi_{n}\to f$ everywhere. Since $m\left(\left[a,b\right]\right)< \infty$, by **Theorem 9.1.15 (Egorov's Theorem)**, for $\varepsilon> 0$ there is a measurable set $B\subset\left[a,b\right]$ with $m\left(B\right)< \varepsilon/3$ such that $\phi_{n}\to f$ uniformly on $\left[a,b\right]\setminus B$. By the simple-function case, for each $n$ choose a compact set $K_{n}\subset\left[a,b\right]$ with $m\left(\left[a,b\right]\setminus K_{n}\right)< \varepsilon/\left(3\cdot2^{n}\right)$ and $\phi_{n}|_{K_{n}}$ continuous.

Set $E:=\left(\left[a,b\right]\setminus B\right)\cap\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}K_{n}$. Then $E$ is an intersection of closed sets in $\left[a,b\right]$, hence compact; and

$$
m\left(\left[a,b\right]\setminus E\right)\le m\left(B\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}m\left(\left[a,b\right]\setminus K_{n}\right)< \varepsilon/3+\varepsilon/3=2\varepsilon/3< \varepsilon.
$$

On $E$, each $\phi_{n}$ is continuous (since $E\subset K_{n}$) and $\phi_{n}\to f$ uniformly (since $E\subset\left[a,b\right]\setminus B$). The uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, so $f|_{E}$ is continuous. $\blacksquare$

## 5

Let $\mathcal E$ be the collection of all finite disjoint unions of rectangles. Prove that $\mathcal E$ is an algebra. For $E=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\times B_{i}$ (pairwise disjoint), define $\pi\left(E\right):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu\left(A_{i}\right)\nu\left(B_{i}\right)$. Prove that $\pi$ is a premeasure on $\mathcal E$, and that the measure on $\sigma\left(\mathcal E\right)$ induced by this premeasure agrees with the product measure defined in **Theorem 10.1.4 (Existence and Uniqueness of the Product Measure)**.

### Solution to 5

**$\mathcal E$ is an algebra.** By Intersection and Complement Properties of Rectangle Algebras, the intersection of measurable rectangles is a measurable rectangle, and the complement of a measurable rectangle is a union of two measurable rectangles. The empty set $\emptyset$ and the whole space $X\times Y$ lie in $\mathcal E$. Taking complements, intersections, and unions of elements of $\mathcal E$, first write each element as a finite disjoint union of rectangles; then, since intersections of rectangles are rectangles and after common refinement they are pairwise disjoint, the result is again a finite disjoint union of rectangles, hence in $\mathcal E$. Thus $\mathcal E$ is an algebra.

**$\pi$ is well-defined.** Let $E=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}\times B_{i}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}C_{j}\times D_{j}$ be two pairwise-disjoint rectangle decompositions of $E$. For the common refinement $\left\{\left(A_{i}\cap C_{j}\right)\times\left(B_{i}\cap D_{j}\right)\right\}_{i,j}$, by finite additivity of $\mu,\nu$,

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu\left(A_{i}\right)\nu\left(B_{i}\right)=\sum_{i,j}\mu\left(A_{i}\cap C_{j}\right)\nu\left(B_{i}\cap D_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu\left(C_{j}\right)\nu\left(D_{j}\right),
$$

so $\pi\left(E\right)$ is independent of the decomposition.

**$\pi$ is a premeasure on $\mathcal E$.** By **Theorem 10.1.4 (Existence and Uniqueness of the Product Measure)**, $\mu\times\nu$ is a measure on $\left(X\times Y,\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B\right)$ with $\left(\mu\times\nu\right)\left(A\times B\right)=\mu\left(A\right)\nu\left(B\right)$ on rectangles; for a finite disjoint union $\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\times B_{i}$, by finite additivity of the measure,

$$
\left(\mu\times\nu\right)\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\times B_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu\left(A_{i}\right)\nu\left(B_{i}\right)=\pi\left(E\right),
$$

so $\pi=\left(\mu\times\nu\right)|_{\mathcal E}$. By The Restriction of a Measure to an Algebra is a Premeasure, $\pi$ satisfies the premeasure conditions: $\pi\left(\emptyset\right)=0$, and for pairwise disjoint $\left\{E_{n}\right\}\subset\mathcal E$ with $\bigcup_{n}E_{n}\in\mathcal E$, by $\sigma$-additivity of $\mu\times\nu$ (**Definition 5.1.2 (Measure)**),

$$
\pi\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\right)=\left(\mu\times\nu\right)\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)\left(E_{n}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\pi\left(E_{n}\right).
$$

Thus $\pi$ is a premeasure on $\mathcal E$ (**Definition 6.2.1 (Premeasure)**).

**Unique extension agrees with the product measure.** By **Definition 10.1.1 (Product $\sigma$-Algebra)**, $\mathcal E$ is generated by all measurable rectangles, so $\sigma\left(\mathcal E\right)=\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B$. Since $\mu,\nu$ are $\sigma$-finite, take $X=\bigcup_{p}X_{p}$, $Y=\bigcup_{q}Y_{q}$ with $\mu\left(X_{p}\right),\nu\left(Y_{q}\right)< \infty$; then the rectangles $X_{p}\times Y_{q}$ cover $X\times Y$ and $\pi\left(X_{p}\times Y_{q}\right)< \infty$, so $\pi$ is $\sigma$-finite. By **Theorem 6.3.3 (Carathéodory's Theorem)** (unique extension of a premeasure), $\pi$ has a unique extension to $\sigma\left(\mathcal E\right)$. This extension agrees with $\pi=\left(\mu\times\nu\right)|_{\mathcal E}$ on $\mathcal E$, hence equals $\mu\times\nu$ on all of $\sigma\left(\mathcal E\right)=\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal B$, i.e. it agrees with the product measure in **Theorem 10.1.4 (Existence and Uniqueness of the Product Measure)**. $\blacksquare$

## 6

Let $X=Y=\left[0,1\right]$, $\mathcal M=\mathcal N=\mathcal B_{\left[0,1\right]}$, $\mu$ be Lebesgue measure and $\nu$ be counting measure. If $D=\left\{\left(x,x\right):x\in\left[0,1\right]\right\}$ is the diagonal in $X\times Y$, then $\iint\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\nu$, $\iint\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,\mathrm{d}\mu$, and $\int\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)$ are not all equal.

### Solution to 6

By **Definition 10.1.2 (Sections)**, the x-section of the diagonal $D$ is $D_{x}=\left\{y:\left(x,y\right)\in D\right\}=\left\{x\right\}$, a singleton.

First compute $\iint\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\nu$ (inner integral w.r.t. $\mu$, outer w.r.t. $\nu$). For fixed $y$,

$$
\int_{X}\chi_{D}\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)=\mu\left(\left\{x:\left(x,y\right)\in D\right\}\right)=\mu\left(\left\{y\right\}\right)=0,
$$

since a singleton has Lebesgue measure zero. Hence $\iint\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\nu=\int_{Y}0\,\mathrm{d}\nu=0$.

Next compute $\iint\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,\mathrm{d}\mu$ (inner w.r.t. $\nu$). For fixed $x$,

$$
\int_{Y}\chi_{D}\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(y\right)=\nu\left(D_{x}\right)=\nu\left(\left\{x\right\}\right)=1,
$$

since a singleton has counting measure $1$. Hence $\iint\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{X}1\,\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)=\mu\left(\left[0,1\right]\right)=1$.

Finally, by **Theorem 10.1.4 (Existence and Uniqueness of the Product Measure)** (construction $\phi\left(E\right)=\int\nu\left(E_{x}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)$),

$$
\int\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\left(\mu\times\nu\right)=\left(\mu\times\nu\right)\left(D\right)=\int_{X}\nu\left(D_{x}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)=\int_{X}\nu\left(\left\{x\right\}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)=\int_{X}1\,\mathrm{d}\mu\left(x\right)=1.
$$

Thus the three integrals are $0$, $1$, $1$, not all equal; in particular $\iint\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\nu=0\ne1=\iint\chi_{D}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,\mathrm{d}\mu$. The reason is that $\nu$ is not $\sigma$-finite, so the $\sigma$-finiteness hypothesis of **Theorem 10.2.1 (Fubini's Theorem)** fails, and the product integral disagrees with the iterated integral first integrating over $\mu$. $\blacksquare$
